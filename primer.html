Pattern primer

Get your ear looking for the pattern

Everything structural from the twenty course lessons, compressed to one page. Not a grammar course — a set of shapes to recognise, so that when Telugu goes past at speed some part of your brain says that's a dative or that's a past tense before the meaning arrives. Read it in fifteen minutes, then come back to the decoder whenever an ending stops making sense.

Part one

The shortcuts

Places where the language lets you off. These are not learner's crutches — every one of them is what native speakers actually do, and the course says so explicitly each time.

The big one

Any English verb + chēyi

Stick chēyi (“do”) after an English word and you have a working Telugu verb. There is no pure-Telugu alternative in common use for most modern concepts.

email chēyaṇḍi — send the email
park chēstānu — I'll park
wait chēyaṇḍi — please wait
pani chēyaṇḍi — the native pattern, “do the work”
The other big one

Any English adjective + -gā

The matching escape hatch for descriptions. Between this and chēyi you are never fully stuck mid-sentence.

sweet gā undi — it's sweet
fast gā veḷtunnāḍu — he's going fast
beautiful gā undi — it's beautiful
Politeness

aṇḍi attaches to almost anything

Telugu keeps politeness in the ending, not in a separate word. There is a word for “please” — dayacēsi — and you should basically never use it.

thanks aṇḍi — the everyday thank-you
avunandi · lēdandi · sarē aṇḍi
rā → raṇḍi — come → please come in
Vocabulary

English nouns take a -u tail

Then they pluralise and decline like any Telugu noun. English profession words are the normal choice, not a fallback — nobody says abhiyanta for engineer.

car → kāru → kārlu
nēnu enjinīrni — I'm an engineer
championlu — champions
When you're stuck

Unsure of a verb form? Negate it.

Two of the three negative tenses are one single form for all eight persons. No agreement, no guessing. The negative costs you eight forms per verb where the positive costs twenty-four.

āḍalēdu — didn't play · anyone, any number
āḍaḍaṁ lēdu — isn't playing · same
Sentence building

No “is / am / are” at all

Put two words next to each other and stop. Add a person marker to the second one if the subject is I, you-informal, or we.

Raj vidyārthi — Raj is a student
idi kāru — this is a car
nēnu vidyārthini — I am a student
Questions

Add -ā to anything

Any statement of any length becomes a yes/no question. No word order change, no auxiliary. The final vowel gives way to the ā rather than sitting beside it.

bāgunnāru → bāgunnārā?
māṭlāḍutundi → māṭlāḍutundā?
Economy

Drop the pronoun

The verb ending already says who. mīru in the standard greeting is optional, and usually dropped.

bāgunnārā? — how are you?
bāgunnānu — I'm fine
Frames

Everything you want starts nāku

Liking, wanting, knowing, being hungry, having a headache — none of them have you as the subject. Learn the frame once and a whole family opens.

nāku X aṇṭē iṣṭaṁ — I like X
nāku X kāvāli — I want X
nāku X vaddu — I don't want X
Kinship

pedda / chinna build the family

“Big” and “small” attach to parent words and generate most of the uncle-and-aunt vocabulary. Grandmothers are just two words stuck together.

pedda + amma → peddamma — mother's elder sister
amma + amma → ammamma — maternal grandmother
The pattern behind the pattern

Telugu has two general-purpose adapters for English: chēyi makes it a verb, -gā makes it a description. Code-mixing is the current colloquial norm in Hyderabad, not sloppiness — thanks aṇḍi is what people say.

Part two

The shape of a sentence

Four facts. Everything else in this page is detail hanging off them.

The verb goes last. Always, in every clause. If you hear a verb in the middle, it's a compound or you misheard. The question word sits immediately before the verb, not at the front: chārminār ekkaḍa undi?
Endings come after the noun. English puts the little word in front — to school, in the bus, with Pooja. Telugu welds it on behind: skūlki, baslō, Pūjatō. That's what “postposition” means.
Word order is free because it's marked. Not loose — marked. The endings say who did what, so the order is available for emphasis instead. Gīta bālni koṭṭindi is “Gita hit the ball”; Gītani bāl koṭṭindi is “the ball hit Gita”. Same words, same order, one suffix moved.
Two vowels never touch. When a suffix would create a vowel clash, one vowel is deleted, the two merge, or a v glides in between. This is why forms look irregular when they aren't: tinu + aṇḍi → tinaṇḍi, rā + aṇḍi → raṇḍi, lē + aṇḍi → lēvaṇḍi. It happens across words too at speed — nēnu ippuḍu → nēnippuḍu — which is why native speech sounds like one long word.
Part three

Reading a verb ending

A Telugu verb is three slots. Once you can hear the seam between them you can decode a form you have never met — which is the whole point of this page.

1 · stemāḍ- The root verb, sometimes slightly reshaped. This is the dictionary word and the informal command.
2 · tense-ā-
-tunnā-
-tā- Past · present · future-or-habit. Three markers, that's all.
3 · person-nu -vu -ḍu
-di -mu -ru -yi Who. Almost always the last syllable of the pronoun itself.
The one rule underneath all of it

The person marker mirrors the last syllable of the pronoun. nēnu → -ānu. nuvvu → -āvu. vāḍu → -āḍu. mīru → -āru. It holds for six of the eight rows outright, and the exceptions are the same exceptions in every tense — so you learn them once, not three times.

Read the shape, not the contents

Two of the three columns are completely flat — one word for all eight subjects. Only the future/habit column inflects, and it uses the bare -nu -vu -ḍu -du -mu -ru set with no tense marker at all. This is the single most useful structural fact in the whole course.

The exceptions, which are always the same three

RowYou'd expectActuallyWhy
she / it-adi-indi · -tōndi · -tundi Contracts. Feminine and non-human share this cell, which makes it the most-used ending in the language.
she / it, negative future-adi-adu Otherwise it would be identical to the pronoun adi sitting right next to it.
avi · they, non-human-avi-āyi Simply irregular. Its negative -avu collides with nuvvu.

Part four

Reading a noun ending

Plurals: -lu / -ulu

The direct equivalent of English -s, and it works on English loanwords too. vidyārthi → vidyārthulu, kāru → kārlu, paṇḍu → paṇḍlu, champion → championlu. When the subject is a plural non-human thing, the verb goes to -āyi / unnāyi — enni paṇḍlu unnāyi?

The two that carry the sentence

-ki / -ku · dative

To, for, toward — destination, recipient. And the subject of anything that happens to you: liking, wanting, knowing, aching.

iṇḍiyāki veḷtunnānu — going to India
Gītaki giphṭ ichchāḍu — gave Gita a gift
nāku telugu rādu — I don't know Telugu

Written Telugu picks between the two spellings by what precedes; spoken treats them as interchangeable, with -ki commoner.

-ni / -nu · accusative

Marks the receiver of the action. No English equivalent, because English uses position instead. This is what frees the word order.

Gīta bālni koṭṭindi — Gita hit the ball
Gītani bāl koṭṭindi — the ball hit Gita

Put it on the wrong noun and you get nonsense rather than an accent: abbāyiki skūlni pampānu means “I sent the school to the boy.”

On a pronoun, use the possessive stem — never the subject form

It's nā + ku → nāku, built on nā (“my”), not on nēnu (“I”). *nēnuku does not exist. This holds for every postposition — nā tō, nā gurinchi, nā kōsaṁ, nā daggara — so one rule covers the whole list and you never need a second table.

Part five

Adjectives and adverbs

Telugu adjectives mostly aren't adjectives — they're nouns wearing a suffix, and which suffix depends on where the word sits. Get this wrong and you produce a phrase that isn't a phrase.

After the noun · -gā

The predicate slot — “it is beautiful”. Needs undi after it.

puvvu andangā undi
పువ్వు అందంగా ఉంది

Before the noun · -aina

The attributive slot — “a beautiful flower”. No verb needed.

andamaina puvvu
అందమైన పువ్వు

Both come off the same noun, andaṁ (“beauty”). Neither is the adjective — the noun is. -ani is a third, smaller ending doing the attributive job: challagā undi but challani nīḷḷu, “cold water”.

A dozen adjectives take no suffix at all

pedda, chinna, kotta, manchi, gaṭṭi, sanna, tīpi, vēḍi, kāraṁ, muriki, chavaka, pēda, cheḍu — these sit in front of a noun bare. pedda illu, kotta kāru. There's no rule that predicts which class a word is in; learn it with the word.

-gā is also how you make an adverb

Same suffix, same logic — it says how. tvaragā quickly, nemmadigā slowly, bāgā well. So Taj Mahal andangā undi is literally “the Taj Mahal exists beautifully”, which is why the verb is required: Telugu describes things by saying how they exist.

The two “is” — and this one is socially loaded

FormClaimsExample
observationundi ఉంది It is that way right now, as I look at it. kāphī vēḍigā undi — this cup is hot
propertyuṇṭundi ఉంటుంది It is that way as a rule — was, is, will be. Hyderabad vēḍigā uṇṭundi — the climate is hot
Where this will bite you

mīru andangā unnāru is “you look beautiful today”. mīru andangā uṇṭāru is “you are a beautiful person, always”. Same for tall, clever, kind. Choosing wrong isn't a grammar error — it changes what you claimed.

Part six

e- asks · i- is near · a- is far

The most elegant pattern in the language, and completely exceptionless. Learn one question word and you get two answers free. English has a broken fragment of this — where / here / there, this / that. Telugu kept the whole thing.

You already know four of these fourteen

ikkaḍa, ippuḍu, idi, adi are all in your deck already, learned as unrelated vocabulary. They're one system, and the other ten come free.

The same shift runs through the pronouns

Every third-person pronoun is the far form. Its near partner opens with i / ī instead of a / ā. Short stays short, long stays long, and neither the meaning nor the register changes.

āyana→īyana · atanu→itanu · vāḍu→vīḍu · āviḍa→īviḍa · āme→īme · adi→idi · vāḷḷu→vīḷḷu · avi→ivi

The other question words

evaru ఎవరుwho ēmiṭi ఏమిటిwhat — when there's no verb, and it goes at the end. mī pēru ēmiṭi?
ēmi ఏమిwhat — when there is a verb, sitting just before it. mīru ēmi chēstāru? ē ఏwhich — before a noun

Rule of thumb: the question word goes immediately before the verb, not at the front. The verb still comes last.

Part seven

Four English verbs Telugu simply doesn't have

These aren't quirks to memorise separately. They're one design decision showing up four times: put the person in an oblique case and let uṇḍu do the work.

EnglishTelugu saysLiterally
to benothing at all“Raj student.” Two words side by side. kādu negates it.
to havenā daggara X undi“near me X exists”. With a person, daggara stops meaning near and means has.
to wantnāku X kāvāli“to me X is wanted”. You're not the subject — it happens to you.
pleasethe -aṇḍi endingPoliteness is grammatical, not lexical. Redirect the instinct into the verb form.

And three more absences worth knowing

no perfect tense“I have eaten” and “I ate” are the same form. Telugu doesn't need the distinction.
no relative pronounsNo which or that clauses. The verb becomes an adjective instead: chimpina chokkā, “a torn shirt” — literally “a tore shirt”. Coming later.
no “where are you from?”You ask where someone comes from, in the habitual: mīru ekkaḍa nunchi vastāru? Switch to the present continuous and it means “where are you coming from right now” — one morpheme apart.
Three words where English has one “no”

lēdu denies existence — there isn't any, I don't have it. vaddu denies wanting — no thanks, don't. kādu denies identity — that's not what it is. Picking wrong changes what you denied. All three take -aṇḍi.

The one that keeps showing up

ani and aṇṭē are the same particle family, and it turns up everywhere: reported speech, aṇṭē in the liking frame, -ālani undi for wanting, endukaṇṭē for because. Five different jobs. If an ending has an- in it and you can't place it, this is probably why.
