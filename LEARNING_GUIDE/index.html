Pronouns & the missing “to be”

Build identity sentences, attach person markers, distinguish inclusive and exclusive “we,” choose respectful pronouns, and generate near forms from distant forms.

 Open lesson 02 PossessivesSelf-introductionFamily & culture

Introducing yourself & the “my / our” split

Fill the identity frame with name, age, profession, nationality, and hometown. Learn nā and mā, why close family always takes “our,” and the fast-speech vowel deletion that makes native speech sound quick.

 Open lesson 03 GreetingsPolitenessNegation

Greetings, politeness & the three ways to say no

Attach aṇḍi to make almost anything respectful, learn why Telugu rarely says “please,” and separate lēdu, vaddu, and kādu — three “no”s that are not interchangeable.

 Open lesson 04 KinshipHonorificsCompounds

Family & kinship names

Telugu splits “uncle” and “aunt” by family side and relative age. Learn the pedda/chinna compounds that generate most of the terms, the gāru honorific, and why atta means both aunt and mother-in-law.

 Open lesson 05 PossessivesRegisterAsking names

Possessive pronouns

Every pronoun has a possessive partner following one of three patterns: unchanged, final vowel shifts, or a completely new word. Adds mī/nī, the inclusive/exclusive “our,” and mī pēru ēmiṭi?

 Open lesson 06 NumbersConstructionLakh & crore

Numbers & how they're built

Ten atoms and four construction rules generate every number you need. Covers the -ai tens, hundreds and thousands, the lakh/crore system used at work, and why Telugu counts people differently from things.

 Open lesson 07 VerbsRoot form-aṇḍi & -vaddu

Command verbs & the root form

Week 2 opens with the hinge of the course: a verb's root is its informal command, and everything else is that root plus stacked suffixes. Thirty verbs, two suffixes, and the vowel rules that join them.

 Open lesson 08 TenseAll pronounsNegation

Present continuous — what you're doing right now

The first tense, and the one that answers "what are you up to?", "where do you work?" and "where do you live?". One marker -tunnā-, a person ending that copies the pronoun's own last syllable, four stem classes, and a negative that turns the verb into a noun.

 Open lesson 09 TenseIrregular stemsNo perfect

Past tense & the stem you have to memorise

Structurally Lesson 8 with one morpheme swapped — but the past stem is often unpredictable from both the root and the present, and nine verbs have to be stored as words. The negative sidesteps all of them. Telugu has no perfect tense and doesn't need one.

 Open lesson 10 TenseHabit = futureTenseless negative

One tense for habit and future

nēnu tennis āḍutānu is both “I play tennis” and “I will play tennis” — Telugu draws no line. The negative drops the tense marker entirely. Carries the two sentences every conversation opens with: where you live and what you do.

 Open lesson 11 Immediate futureFused verbQuotative ani

“About to” — the one place Telugu copies English

-bōtunnānu is the verb pō “go” glued on with p softened to b — the same metaphor English used to build “going to”. Uses the plain stem, so every irregularity disappears. Also your first sighting of the quotative ani.

 Open lesson 12 Adjectivesundi vs uṇṭundiThe -gā suffix

Adjectives & the two ways to say “is”

Telugu splits English “is” in two: undi for what you observe right now, uṇṭundi for what is true as a rule. Hot coffee vs a hot city; a good mood vs a cheerful person. Plus -gā, which turns any noun — or any English adjective — into a description.

 Open lesson 13 RecapFull gridsPDF audit

Recap — every tense in two tables

Weeks 8–12 on one page: two suffix tables, six stem classes, and one rule. Every grid is generated from the audited stem inventory, and the 880-cell diff against the course's own conjugation PDF is here too — including the error it caught.

 Open lesson 14 Nounskāya vs paṇḍuPlural agreement

Nouns — colours, food, animals, flowers

93 nouns, and the pattern the word-list videos never mention: Telugu splits produce into kāya and paṇḍu, unripe and ripe, not into vegetable and fruit. Plus the nine mass nouns that are grammatically plural and change the verb.

 Open lesson 15 Adjectives-gā vs -aina19 new verbs

Two slots for a description, and 19 new verbs

Lesson 12's -gā cannot go in front of a noun — that slot needs -aina, and a dozen adjectives need no suffix at all. Four entries in the source list are not adjectives; each mistake is a grammar point. The Verb Lab is now 54 verbs and 3,282 forms.

 Open lesson 16 QuestionsThe -ā particlee- / i- / a-

Questions — one vowel, then one letter

Any statement becomes a yes/no question by adding -ā. And every question word starts e-, with a near answer in i- and a far one in a- — seven complete triples, no exceptions. English kept only the fragments where/here/there.

 Open lesson 17 PostpositionsDative subjectFree word order

Two suffixes that carry the whole sentence

In Telugu you cannot want something — wanting happens to you, and the sentence says so with nāku. And -ni marks who a verb happens to, which is why word order can move: Gīta bālni koṭṭindi and Gītani bāl koṭṭindi differ by one suffix and mean opposite things.

 Open lesson 18 PostpositionsNo verb “have”tana

The easy six — and one that isn’t

Six that map straight onto English, nine more listed without comment, and daggara — which is not vocabulary but the fact that Telugu has no verb “to have”. Things exist near you. Pairs with last lesson: you cannot want, and you cannot have.

 Open lesson 19 ModalsOnly two inflectlēdu conjugates

Can, must, want — three suffixes and their negatives

Six suffixes on the same root you have been drilling since Lesson 7. Only can and cannot change with person. And “want to” is not a modal at all — it is must plus is, with the dative subject from Lesson 17.

 Open lesson 20 Conjunctionsaṇṭē solved-ū / -ō

Joining clauses — and the word hiding in five lessons

“And” and “or” can be suffixes, and the verb’s agreement tells you which was used. Then the payoff: aṇṭē is the conditional of anu “to say” — a cell the Verb Lab has generated since Lesson 7 — which closes five loose ends at once. “Because” is literally “if you ask why”.

Learning method

Map it. Hear it. Retrieve it.

The lesson pages are the structural spine, not the whole practice system. Every pattern should eventually be recognized in speech, produced from memory, and used for a personally meaningful message.

01 / Map

See every meaningful piece

Separate subjects, stems, plural endings, person markers, and sound changes so the pattern becomes visible.

02 / Retrieve

Rebuild without the diagram

Use the self-test and Anki inventory to retrieve complete forms rather than merely recognizing explanations.

03 / Use

Connect the pattern to speech

Listen for the form, create true sentences, record them, and use native-speaker attention for pronunciation and naturalness checks.
