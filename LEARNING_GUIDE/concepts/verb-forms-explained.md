# What all the verb form names actually mean

Written in answer to a real question: *"When I see 'present continuous' that makes sense — but
I don't really know the concepts even in English."* Not yet styled as a site page; the content
is the point.

## The names are coordinates, not labels

"Present continuous" isn't one thing. It's two dimensions stacked:

| Dimension | The question it answers | English examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Tense** | *When?* | past / present / future |
| **Aspect** | *What shape is the event in time?* | finished / ongoing / habitual |
| **Mood** | *What's your stance?* | statement / command / obligation / ability / hypothetical |
| **Polarity** | *Is it or isn't it?* | positive / negative |
| **Person** | *Who?* | I / you / he / we / they |

Every form name is a point in that space. "Present continuous" = present tense + continuous
aspect. "Negative past" = past tense + negative polarity.

**Aspect is the one you use constantly and have never named.** All present tense:

- "I eat rice" — habitual. Says nothing about right now.
- "I am eating rice" — progressive. Right now, mid-action.

All past tense:

- "I ate" — simple, finished, sealed.
- "I was eating" — ongoing back then.
- "I have eaten" — finished, but relevant *now*.
- "I had eaten" — finished before some other past thing.

You choose between those flawlessly and unconsciously. English marks aspect with helper words
(*am, have, had, was*); Telugu marks it with endings.

## The 14 forms in the Verb Lab

### Statements — tense + aspect + polarity

| Form | Telugu | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| Habitual & future | `chēstānu` | **Both** "I do" and "I will do" — Telugu draws no line |
| Present continuous | `chēstunnānu` | Right now, mid-action |
| Past | `chēsānu` | "I did" *and* usually "I have done" |
| Negative habitual & future | `chēyanu` | "I don't / I won't" |
| Negative past | `chēyalēdu` | "I didn't". Same for every person |

Two things here differ from English more than anything else in the language:

1. **Present and future are one form.** Context decides.
2. **Negation is not a helper word.** English bolts on "not"; Telugu switches to a *different
   stem*: `chēs-` → `chēya-`. That's why the negative feels unfamiliar — it isn't the positive
   plus something, it's a separate shape.

### Commands — mood + register + polarity

| Form | Telugu | |
|---|---|---|
| Imperative, familiar | `chēyi` | children, close friends only |
| Imperative, polite | `chēyaṇḍi` | your default |
| Prohibitive, familiar | `chēyaku` | "don't" |
| Prohibitive, polite | `chēyakaṇḍi` | |

Four cells because two dimensions cross: positive/negative × familiar/polite. **Telugu
grammaticalises politeness** — it's an ending, not a word like "please".

*(Note: Lesson 7 teaches `-vaddu` for the prohibitive — `chēyavaddu` — rather than `-ku`. Both
are real and they differ in force. See the flag in that lesson.)*

### Modality — your stance

| Form | Telugu | |
|---|---|---|
| Hortative | `chēddāṁ` | "let's" |
| Obligation | `chēyāli` | "must / have to" |
| Ability | `chēyagalanu` | "can" |

English uses separate modal verbs; Telugu suffixes them.

### Not tenses at all — clause connectors

| Form | Telugu | |
|---|---|---|
| Purposive | `chēyaḍāniki` | "in order to" |
| Conditional | `chēstē` | "if" |

These glue two clauses together. English uses standalone words ("to", "if"); Telugu uses an
ending on the first verb. That's why they feel odd in a conjugation table — they're doing a
conjunction's job.

## Why there seem to be so many

There aren't. Count what English needs for the same jobs: *will, am, was, have, had, do not,
did not, must, can, let's, if, to, please.* English has just as many constructions — it spreads
them over separate words, so you never had to memorise them as a list.

Telugu is **agglutinative**: it stacks that meaning onto the end of the verb. `chēyakaṇḍi` is
one word doing what "please don't do it" does in four. Identical information, different
packaging.

Which is why the stem/ending colour-coding matters. The endings are a small fixed set that
never changes across all 35 verbs. Only the stems vary. Once the endings are automatic, a new
verb costs five stems, not forty forms.

## Three forms deliberately left out of the Verb Lab

All three are **multi-word constructions**, not a stem plus an ending — so they don't fit the
generator. But the deeper reason is that each one's English name is a trap:

- **Perfect** (`chēsi unnānu`) — Telugu's perfect is *not* where English's "have done" goes.
  Most of the time English "I have eaten" is just the plain past, `tinnānu`. Generating a
  "perfect" column would teach a false equivalence.
- **Completive `-ēyi`** (`visirēstānu`) — very common in Hyderabad speech, but it's *vēyi*
  ("throw") fused on as an auxiliary, and where the fusion lands varies per verb. Its meaning
  is a subtle "get it done", not a tense.
- **Past continuous** — Telugu genuinely varies, and colloquial Telangana often just uses the
  plain past where English says "I was doing".

You have already met one of this family: `alisipōyānu` ("I'm a bit tired") in the scenario
deck, which the site tells you to memorise as a block. That's `pōvu` as an auxiliary.

## What exists beyond this, so you know where the map ends

Safely ignorable for months, but real:

- **Relative / participial forms** — `chēsina pani`, "the work that was done". Telugu has no
  "which/that" clauses; it turns the verb into an adjective. Big one, needed eventually.
- **The `-tū` converb** — "while doing": `tinṭū māṭlāḍutānu`.
- **The quotative `ani`** — how reported speech works. Common, very unlike English.
- **Causatives** — you already have these: `nērchukō` (learn) vs `nērpinchu` (teach).
- **Passive** — exists, rare in speech, genuinely ignorable.

## Priority

Of the 14, the five statement forms carry the overwhelming majority of real conversation, and
the polite imperative gets you through most interactions. That's the "core six" default in the
Verb Lab, and it's the right place to stay until the endings come without hesitation.
