Can, must, want — three suffixes and their negatives

English needs separate words for these: can, must, should, want to. Telugu has six suffixes, all attaching to the same root you have been drilling since Lesson 7.

Two of them inflect for person and four do not, which is the rule to hold onto. And want turns out not to be a modal at all — it is must plus is, and it takes the dative subject you met last lesson.

How I processed this

-galanu arrives as "galeno", then as "Gullah, Gullah, Gullah, Gullah" when the instructor runs the person markers. -kūḍadu appears as "kudzu", "koudou" and "acuerdo"; chēyalēnu is "Jay Leno" throughout.

This video changed the Verb Lab. It had can stored as person-invariant, which the video contradicts outright. See the audit.

The rule to hold onto

Only can and cannot change with person

The other four are one form for every subject. The video states this outright and it is worth taking at face value — it halves the work.

cannot is lēdu wearing person markers — again

chēyalēnu · chēyalēvu · chēyalēḍu · chēyalēdu · chēyalēru. That is exactly the set you met in Lesson 13 for uṇḍu: lēnu, lēvu, lēḍu, lēdu, lēmu, lēru.

It is the same word doing the same thing. lēdu is not a fixed particle — it is a defective verb meaning "is not", and it conjugates. Once you see that, three separate things you have learned collapse into one: the negative past, uṇḍu's negative, and "cannot".

And the person endings on both are the plain -nu / -vu / -ḍu / -du / -ru set from the negative future — a fourth place they turn up.

Not actually a modal

"Want to" is must + is

Take the obligation form, add ani, add undi. Literally: "it is that I should."

chēyā-+ -li+ ani+ undi → chēyālani undi

That ani is the quotative for the fifth time — after RRR ani sinimā, reported speech, aṇṭē in the liking frame, and now this. It is quietly one of the most productive particles in the language, and the course still has not taught it.

It takes a dative subject, like every other wanting

nāku chēyālani undi — never *nēnu chēyālani undi. Same as nāku kāvāli and nāku iṣṭaṁ from Lesson 17.

So "want" is consistent across the whole language: wanting a thing is nāku X kāvāli, wanting to do something is nāku X-ālani undi. Both put you in the dative because in Telugu, wanting is not something you do.

The exercise the video sets — and it is a good one

Say your actual goals, three ways: nēnu telugu nērchukōgalanu (I can), nēnu rōju telugu chaduvukōvāli (I must, every day), nāku telugu nērchukōvālani undi (I want to).

Three sentences about the thing you are actually doing right now, using three different modals. Put them in your free-production block tonight.

Quick self-test

  • Which two of the six modals change with person?can and cannot. The other four are one form for every subject.
  • "I can speak Telugu" and "you can speak Telugu".nēnu telugu māṭlāḍagalanu. / nuvvu telugu māṭlāḍagalavu.
  • Where have you seen lēnu / lēvu / lēḍu / lēru before?uṇḍu's negative, Lesson 13. lēdu is a defective verb that conjugates — same word both times.
  • Break chēyālani undi into its parts.chēyāli (must) + ani (quotative) + undi (is) = "it is that I should" = I want to.
  • Why is it nāku and not nēnu for wanting?Wanting is not something you do in Telugu — it happens to you. Same as nāku kāvāli.
  • "I want to learn Telugu."nāku telugu nērchukōvālani undi.
  • Why the v in nērchukōvāli?-ō + ā would be two vowels together. A v glides in, same as lē → lēvaṇḍi.
  • "I shouldn't do that" — which suffix?-kūḍadu. chēyakūḍadu. Stronger than the prohibitive -ku: "ought not", not "don't".
  • Turn "you can do yoga" into a question.nuvvu yōgā chēyagalavā? — the -ā particle from Lesson 16.
  • Say one real goal three ways: can, must, want to.nēnu ___ galanu. / nēnu ___ āli. / nāku ___ ālani undi.
