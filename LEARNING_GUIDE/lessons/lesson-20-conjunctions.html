Joining clauses — and the word that was hiding in five lessons

Seven conjunctions, two of which are suffixes rather than words. Straightforward on its own terms.

But three of them contain aṇṭē, and aṇṭē is not a conjunction. It is a cell in a paradigm you have been drilling since Lesson 7 — and once you see which one, five loose ends from five different lessons close at once.

How I processed this

The most damaged transcript in the course. kabaṭṭi survives only as "cuppa tea" and "kava tea"; endukaṇṭē as "yenikent"; the whole final example, kāvālaṇṭē tvaragā raṇḍi, as "Cavalcante Tverraga Randy". The -ō suffix demonstration collapses into "Narasimha Naidu".

Almost everything here was rebuilt from the grammar rather than the audio. That is safe because the pieces are all forms the Verb Lab already generates — see below.

Two ways each

"And" and "or" can be suffixes

Telugu will let you use a word — mariyu, lēdā — but in speech it more often attaches a vowel to both items.

TeluguScriptVerb
andsuffix -ū on bothRāmū Śyāmū naḍustunnāruరామూ శ్యామూ నడుస్తున్నారుplural
andthe wordRām mariyu Śyām naḍustunnāruరామ్ మరియు శ్యామ్ నడుస్తున్నారుplural
orsuffix -ō on bothRāmō Śyāmō naḍustunnāḍuరామో శ్యామో నడుస్తున్నాడుsingular
The verb tells you which one it was

-ū makes two people into a plural subject, so the verb takes -āru. -ō means only one of them is doing it, so the verb stays singular, -āḍu.

That is a second, redundant signal — the suffix and the agreement say the same thing. Useful when the vowel gets lost in fast speech, which it will.

Note that both suffixes go on both items, unlike English where the conjunction sits between them once. And note the shape: -ū and -ō are single vowels, so they merge with whatever they attach to — Rām + ū → Rāmū.

lēdā is for a genuine choice between two named options:

mīku ṭī kāvālā lēdā kāphī kāvālā? మీకు టీ కావాలా లేదా కాఫీ కావాలా? — do you want tea or coffee?

Note the whole question is built from Lesson 16's -ā particle and Lesson 17's mīku ... kāvāli. Nothing here is new except the joining word.

The payoff

aṇṭē is the conditional of "to say"

You have met this particle five times without being told what it is. Here is what it is.

anu· to say — Verb Lab entry since Lesson 7
anu+ -tē (conditional)→ aṇṭē "if one says"

The Verb Lab already generates it. anu has been in the 35 since the first verb lesson, and its cond cell is aṇṭē. Every appearance of this particle across five lessons has been one table cell of one ordinary verb.

Where you met itLiterally
L11RRR ani sinimā"RRR", saying — a film called RRR
L11vastānu ani cheppāḍu"I'll come", saying, he said
L17nāku krikeṭ aṇṭē iṣṭaṁif one says cricket — to me, liked
L19nāku chēyālani undithat I should do — it is
L20endukaṇṭēif one asks why — because
L20kāvālaṇṭēif one says "want" — if you want
So "because" is a question with an answer attached

endukaṇṭē is enduku ("why", Lesson 16) + aṇṭē ("if one asks"). Telugu's word for because is literally "if you ask why" — the speaker anticipates the question and answers it.

And kāvālaṇṭē is kāvāli + aṇṭē: "if [you] say you want". That is how Telugu builds "if" clauses with anything that is not a plain verb.

For plain verbs, "if" is the -tē conditional directly — chēstē "if I do", vastē "if he comes". That paradigm is in the Verb Lab and has been flagged conf:'check' since it was built. This lesson is the confirmation it was waiting for.

vēḍi kāphī kāvālaṇṭē tvaragā raṇḍi వేడి కాఫీ కావాలంటే త్వరగా రండి
— if you want hot coffee, come soon

vēḍi from Lesson 12, kāvāli from Lesson 17, raṇḍi from Lesson 7. Every word in that sentence is something you already have.

The remaining four

but · that · therefore · because

TeluguMeaning
Rām naḍustunnāḍu kānī Śyām parugettutunnāḍu
రామ్ నడుస్తున్నాడు కానీ శ్యామ్ పరుగెత్తుతున్నాడు		Ram is walking but Shyam is running.kānī sits between the clauses, exactly like English.
nēnu rōju jāging veḷtānu ani Jān annāḍu
నేను రోజు జాగింగ్ వెళ్తాను అని జాన్ అన్నాడు		John said he goes jogging every day.Reversed. See below.
Gīta iṇṭiki vastōndi kābaṭṭi Rāj kāphī chēsāḍu
గీత ఇంటికి వస్తోంది కాబట్టి రాజ్ కాఫీ చేసాడు		Gita is coming home, therefore Raj made coffee.Same two clauses, opposite order — mirror images, as in English.
Rāj kāphī chēsāḍu endukaṇṭē Gīta iṇṭiki vastōndi
రాజ్ కాఫీ చేసాడు ఎందుకంటే గీత ఇంటికి వస్తోంది		Raj made coffee because Gita is coming home.
ani runs backwards from English "that" English: John said that he goes jogging — the marker comes before the quoted content. Telugu puts the content first and tags it: nēnu rōju jāging veḷtānu ani Jān annāḍu, roughly "I go jogging every day," is what John said.

And notice the quote stays in the first person — nēnu, not "he". Telugu quotes verbatim rather than shifting the pronoun. That is the single most alien thing about reported speech here, and it is why ani deserved a lesson of its own long before this one.

Quick self-test

  • "Ram and Shyam are walking" — twice, one with a word and one with a suffix.Rām mariyu Śyām naḍustunnāru. / Rāmū Śyāmū naḍustunnāru.
  • Change it to "Ram or Shyam is walking". Two things change.Rāmō Śyāmō naḍustunnāḍu. — the suffix, and the verb drops to singular.
  • What is aṇṭē, grammatically?The conditional of anu, "to say" — "if one says". Already a cell in the Verb Lab.
  • Break down endukaṇṭē.enduku (why) + aṇṭē (if one says) = "if you ask why" = because.
  • "John said he goes jogging every day."nēnu rōju jāging veḷtānu ani Jān annāḍu — content first, ani after, and the quote stays in the first person.
  • Why is nēnu in that sentence when John is the subject?Telugu quotes verbatim. It doesn't shift the pronoun the way English does.
  • Say the same fact twice, with kābaṭṭi and with endukaṇṭē.Cause first + kābaṭṭi + effect. / Effect first + endukaṇṭē + cause. Mirror images.
  • "If you want hot coffee, come soon."vēḍi kāphī kāvālaṇṭē tvaragā raṇḍi. — every word already known.
  • How do you say "if" with a plain verb, rather than with kāvāli?The -tē conditional directly: chēstē, vastē. Drillable in the Verb Lab.
