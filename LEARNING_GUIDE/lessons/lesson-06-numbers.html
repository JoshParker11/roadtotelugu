Numbers & how they're built

Telugu numbers are unusually systematic. Learn ten atoms and four construction rules and you can produce any number you'll realistically need. This lesson also covers two things English speakers consistently trip on: the lakh/crore system and the fact that Telugu counts people differently from things.

How I built this one

You said the source only counts a few numbers, so unlike Lessons 1–5 this isn't a transcript extraction — I've written it from general Telugu knowledge to give you the full system and its patterns. That changes the confidence level: treat this lesson as a well-informed draft rather than course-verified material.

The core (1–10, the teens, the tens, hundreds and thousands) is standard and low-risk. The parts most worth checking with your wife are the 101–199 combining form (rule 06), the human counting forms (rule 09), and which ordinal endings she actually uses (rule 10). I've flagged each inline.

The grammar engine

Structures & patterns

Colour code: number word · multiplier / base · changed or added part. Rules 03–07 are the construction system — they're what turn ten memorised words into every number you need.

Ten atoms — these must be memorised

There's no pattern hiding in 1–10. Everything else in the language is built from these, so this is the one block that genuinely requires rote work.

Zero is sunnā సున్నా — the same word also means a circle or a nought.

You already know okaṭi — and its other form

This is the oka deep dive resurfacing as a general rule. The number "one" has two shapes depending on whether it stands alone or sits before a noun.

FormUseExample
okaṭi ఒకటిStanding alone — counting, answering "how many"okaṭi, reṇḍu, mūḍu…
oka ఒకBefore a nounoka pustakaṁ — "one book"

Crucially, the other numbers don't do this. reṇḍu, mūḍu and the rest keep one form in both positions — reṇḍu kārlu, "two cars." Only "one" splits, which is exactly why it developed its article-like uses.

Every ten from 20 to 90 ends in -ai

The single most useful pattern in the lesson. You already have two of these from Lesson 2 — iravai (20) and muppai (30).

Several visibly echo their unit — tommidi → tombhai, enimidi → enabhai. That's a recognition aid rather than a production rule: don't try to derive them, but do let the resemblance help you remember which is which.

21–99: just put the two words together

No connector, no changes. Ten word followed by unit word. This is where the system pays off — eight tens plus nine units covers everything from 21 to 99.

iravai20 + aidu5 → iravai aidu
iravai okaṭi ఇరవై ఒకటి — 21
muppai ēḍu ముప్పై ఏడు — 37
tombhai tommidi తొంభై తొమ్మిది — 99

This makes your age from Lesson 2 immediately expressible at any value: nā vayasu nalabhai reṇḍu, "my age is 42."

Hundreds and thousands: multiplier + base

Same juxtaposition logic, one level up. The base word pluralises when multiplied.

ValueTeluguRomanizedStructure
100వందvandabase word
200రెండు వందలుreṇḍu vandalu2 × hundred (plural -lu)
500ఐదు వందలుaidu vandalu5 × hundred
1,000వెయ్యిveyyibase word
2,000రెండు వేలుreṇḍu vēlu2 × thousand (veyyi → vēlu)

Note the plural -lu ending — the same one from Lesson 1 that gave you vidyārthulu and kārlu. Numbers aren't a special system; they're nouns behaving like nouns.

For values between, chain the parts largest first: reṇḍu vēlu ēḍu vandalu = 2,700.

Verify: 101–199 For numbers just past 100, the traditional combining form is nūṭa నూట — so 150 is nūṭa yābhai and 101 is nūṭa okaṭi. You may also hear vanda used directly. Worth asking which your wife says, since this is the one spot in the system where I'd expect regional and generational variation.

Lakh and crore — the part that will actually catch you out

India doesn't group large numbers in thousands and millions. It uses lakh (100,000) and crore (10,000,000), and this carries straight into English conversation in Hyderabad.

TeluguRomanizedValueWritten
వెయ్యిveyyi1,0001,000
లక్షlakṣa100 thousand1,00,000
కోటిkōṭi10 million1,00,00,000

Notice the digit grouping in that last column. Indian convention groups the last three digits, then twos: 12,34,567 rather than 1,234,567. You'll see this in spreadsheets, payslips, and news.

Why this is genuinely work-relevant Budgets, salaries, headcounts and populations get quoted in lakhs and crores by default — including when your colleagues are speaking English. "Fifty lakh" is 5,000,000 and "two crore" is 20,000,000. Getting fluent in this conversion is arguably more immediately useful to you than any other item in this lesson, and it costs you two words.

Counting things: number before the noun

Straightforward, and it reuses the plural you already know.

reṇḍu kārlu రెండు కార్లు — "two cars"
mūḍu pustakālu — "three books"

The noun takes its plural form, exactly as in Lesson 1. There's no separate counting word for objects, which makes this the easy half of the system.

Counting people uses different words

Here's the part that surprises English speakers. Telugu's human / non-human split — the one behind vāḷḷu versus avi in Lesson 1 — extends to counting. You cannot use the ordinary numbers for people.

ThingsPeople
1okaṭiokaru ఒకరు
2reṇḍuiddaru ఇద్దరు
3mūḍumugguru ముగ్గురు
4nālugunaluguru నలుగురు
5aiduaiduguru ఐదుగురు

Notice the shared -ru / -guru ending — the same -ru that marks respectful plurals in mīru and vāḷḷu. Beyond about five, the general pattern uses mandi మంది, a counter word for people:

padi mandi పది మంది — "ten people"
iravai mandi — "twenty people"

This matters for you directly: describing a team, a meeting, or a family means counting people, not objects. Mā ṭīm lō padi mandi — "there are ten people on my team."

Verify: the exact cutover The -guru forms are standard for the low numbers, and mandi takes over for larger counts — but where speakers switch, and whether they'd say aiduguru or aidu mandi for five, varies. Ask your wife what sounds natural to her.

Ordinals: first, second, third

modaṭi మొదటి ("first") is its own word. After that, ordinals are formed from the number.

TeluguRomanized
firstమొదటిmodaṭi irregular
secondరెండవ / రెండోreṇḍava / reṇḍō
thirdమూడవ / మూడోmūḍava / mūḍō
fourthనాలుగవ / నాలుగోnālugava / nālugō

Two endings, same meaning: -ava is the fuller form, -ō the shortened colloquial one. Once you know either, every ordinal above "first" follows.

Verify: which ending she uses Both -ava and -ō are correct, but everyday Hyderabad speech tends toward the shorter form. Worth confirming before you pick one to drill.

Money, and asking the price

Combine numbers with rūpāyalu రూపాయలు ("rupees") and you can complete the transaction frame from Lesson 3.

idi enta? — "How much is this?"
yābhai rūpāyalu. యాభై రూపాయలు. — "Fifty rupees."
vanda rūpāyalu. — "A hundred rupees."

That's a complete exchange built from three lessons: the question from Lesson 3, the number from this one, and the zero-copula sentence shape from Lesson 1.

Telugu has its own digits — but nobody writes them

Telugu script includes numerals: ౦ ౧ ౨ ౩ ౪ ౫ ౬ ౭ ౮ ౯ for 0–9. You may see them in traditional or decorative contexts.

In practice, Arabic numerals are used everywhere — signs, prices, phone numbers, documents. This is recognition-only trivia, not something to learn actively. Mentioned so you're not thrown if you encounter them.

The efficient path through this lesson

If you learn only three things here, make them: 1–10, the eight tens, and lakh/crore. That's twenty items and it covers ages, prices, quantities, phone digits, and every large figure you'll hear at work. The teens, human-counting forms, and ordinals can follow later without blocking anything.

Quick self-test

Most of these are construction problems, not recall. Cover the green line and assemble the answer first.

  • Say 42.nalabhai reṇḍu.   (ten word + unit, no connector)
  • Say 99.tombhai tommidi.
  • Say 300.mūḍu vandalu.   (multiplier + plural base)
  • Say 2,700.reṇḍu vēlu ēḍu vandalu.   (largest part first)
  • Give your age using the Lesson 2 frame — pick any number.nā vayasu ______.   e.g. nā vayasu muppai āru = 36
  • "How much is this?" — then answer "fifty rupees."idi enta? / yābhai rūpāyalu.
  • "Two cars" and "two people" — why are they different?reṇḍu kārlu / iddaru.   People take the special -ru counting forms.
  • "Ten people."padi mandi.   (mandi is the counter for larger groups)
  • How much is "fifty lakh" in plain digits?5,000,000.   A lakh is 100,000.
  • How much is "two crore"?20,000,000.   A crore is 10,000,000.
  • Which number has two forms, and why?okaṭi (alone) / oka (before a noun) — which is how oka became article-like.
