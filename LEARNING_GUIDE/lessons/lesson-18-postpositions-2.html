The easy six — and one that isn't

Six postpositions that behave almost exactly like their English counterparts, plus nine more listed without comment. You could learn them in ten minutes.

Then there is daggara, which the video treats as a vocabulary item with two meanings. It is not. It is how Telugu does something English needs a verb for — and Telugu does not have that verb.

How I processed this

gurinchi is transcribed as "Gurindji" and then, repeatedly, as "guarantee". daggara appears as "dagora", "de guerra" and "jugara"; chuṭṭū as "Q2". kōsaṁ came through unscathed, which after four weeks is worth remarking on.

The nine place-and-time suffixes at the end are given as a bare list with no examples, so the examples on this page are mine, built from vocabulary already in the masters.

The straightforward ones

The rule from last lesson holds for every one of them

On a pronoun, use the possessive stem: nā lō, nā tō, nā gurinchi, nā kōsaṁ, nā daggara. Never *nēnu tō. The instructor repeats this four separate times in fourteen minutes, which tells you what learners get wrong.

So you do not need six new pronoun tables. One rule covers all of them, and you already learned it.

The one that isn't

Telugu has no verb "to have"

The video says daggara has "two meanings — near, and something like have". That undersells it. There is no Telugu verb for having. Possession is expressed as location: the thing exists near you.

TeluguLiterallyMeans
nā daggara pilli undiనా దగ్గర పిల్లి ఉందిnear me a cat existsI have a cat
Rāj daggara nā pustakaṁ undiరాజ్ దగ్గర నా పుస్తకం ఉందిnear Raj my book existsRaj has my book
vāḷḷa daggara nī pustakaṁ undiవాళ్ళ దగ్గర నీ పుస్తకం ఉందిnear them your book existsthey have your book
With a person, daggara stops meaning "near" Rāj daggara nā pustakaṁ undi does not mean "my book is near Raj". It means Raj has it. The instructor is explicit: daggara is never used with people to mean near. Proximity to a person needs a different word — pakkana, "beside".
This is the second half of a pair, and you have the first

Last lesson: you cannot want in Telugu — wanting happens to you, nāku. This lesson: you cannot have in Telugu — things exist near you, nā daggara.

Both English verbs are missing, and both are replaced by putting you in an oblique case and letting uṇḍu do the work. That is not two quirks; it is one design decision showing up twice.

Telugu also uses the dative for a second kind of having — nāku talanoppi undi, "I have a headache". The rough split: daggara for things you could put down, nāku for things you cannot. The course does not draw this line; worth one question.

Answering an old question

tana — and why tana venaka finally makes sense

Three sentences in this video use tana, and the video calls it "himself or herself" in passing.

TeluguMeaning
Gīta tana annatō skūlki veḷḷindi
గీత తన అన్నతో స్కూల్‌కి వెళ్ళింది		Gita went to school with her own brother.
John tana phreṇḍtō māṭlāḍāḍu
John తన ఫ్రెండ్‌తో మాట్లాడాడు		John spoke with his own friend.
Gīta tana graduation kōsaṁ Ḍhillī veḷḷindi
గీత తన graduation కోసం ఢిల్లీ వెళ్ళింది		Gita went to Delhi for her own graduation.
The card you asked about in week one

tana turned up early as a bare vocabulary card glossed "his", with the example tana venaka and no explanation. That gloss was wrong enough that it is now corrected in data/overrides_words.tsv.

tana points back at the subject of its own sentence. Gīta tana annatō is Gita's own brother. Gīta atani annatō would be someone else's brother. English collapses both into "her", which is why the distinction is invisible until Telugu forces it.

And venaka is in the list at the bottom of this very lesson — "behind". So tana venaka is "behind himself". Both halves of that card are now taught.

A dead suffix

yokka — the apostrophe-s nobody says

Exactly equivalent to English 's. Essentially never used in speech.

Gīta yokka kāru·formal, written, advertising
Gīta kāru·what anyone actually says

Telugu marks possession by putting the two nouns next to each other and nothing else. The instructor's example of where yokka survives is television advertising — Colgate yokka toothpaste — where the register is deliberately precise.

Learn it to recognise. Using it in conversation would sound like saying "the automobile belonging to Gita".

Listed without examples

Nine more, for place and time

The video reads these out and stops. They follow the same two rules as everything above: after the noun, possessive stem on a pronoun. Examples below are mine.

on, abovemīdaమీదballa mīda — on the table
under, belowkindaకిందballa kinda
in front of, beforemunduముందుiṇṭi mundu
behindvenakaవెనకtana venaka
insidelōpalaలోపలiṇṭi lōpala
outsidebayaṭaబయటiṇṭi bayaṭa
beside, next topakkanaపక్కనnā pakkana — beside me
aroundchuṭṭūచుట్టూiṇṭi chuṭṭū
until, up tovarakuవరకుrēpaṭi varaku — until tomorrow
Two of these are the ones you need for people daggara is taken — it means possession with a person. To say someone is physically near you, use pakkana ("beside") or venaka / mundu. nā pakkana kūrchōṇḍi — "sit beside me".

The video closes by pointing out that this list has no natural end — Telugu is agglutinative, so there are as many suffixes as there are relations to express. These fifteen cover the great majority of what you will need, and the two rules that govern them do not change.

Quick self-test

  • "I'm going from Chennai to Delhi."nēnu Chennai nunchi Ḍhillī veḷtunnānu.
  • "with me" and "about me" — careful with the stem.nā tō and nā gurinchi. Possessive stem, never nēnu.
  • "I have a cat." There is no verb for it.nā daggara pilli undi — "near me a cat exists".
  • Does Rāj daggara nā pustakaṁ undi mean my book is near Raj?No — Raj has it. With a person, daggara means possession, never proximity.
  • So how do you say someone is physically near you?nā pakkana — beside me. daggara is taken.
  • What do "I want water" and "I have a cat" have in common?Neither uses the English verb, because Telugu has neither. Both put you in an oblique case and let uṇḍu do the work.
  • Difference between Gīta tana annatō and Gīta atani annatō?Her own brother vs someone else's. tana points back at the subject; English collapses both into "her".
  • Say "Gita's car" the way a person would.Gīta kāru. Just juxtapose. yokka is written-only.
  • "It's on the table" and "it's under the table".balla mīda undi. / balla kinda undi.
  • Name five things you have, and three you want.nā daggara ___ undi ×5, nāku ___ kāvāli ×3. Two different constructions, neither with an English-style verb.
