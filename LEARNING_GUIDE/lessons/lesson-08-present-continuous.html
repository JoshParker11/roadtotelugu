Present continuous — what you're doing right now

Lesson 7 gave you the roots. This is the first thing you can do with them, and it is the tense you will use most: every answer to "what are you up to?" and every sentence about where you work and where you live. One suffix, -tunnā-, plus a person marker that turns out to be a copy of the pronoun's own last syllable.

How I processed this

Two videos merged into one page: video 9 teaches the tense in the first person, video 10 extends it across every pronoun. They are one lesson split for length — the second is not new grammar, it is the rest of the table.

The captions were the worst yet. The negative suffix -ḍaṁ lēdu is transcribed as "Dumbledore" throughout, pani chēstunnā- comes through as "Upanishad" and "pernicious", and āḍutunnāvu as "Art Nouveau". Full list below. Every form on this page was rebuilt from the root-verb PDF and the Verb Lab stems, then checked against tools/te2rom.py, so the Telugu script and the romanization agree by construction.

Raw transcripts are kept at sources/transcripts/week-02/ so you can see exactly what I was working from.

The grammar engine

Structures & rules introduced

Colour code: stem · tense marker · person marker. Nine rules, but rules 1 and 5 are the whole lesson — the rest are consequences.

The suffix is two morphemes, not one

The lesson gives you -tunnānu as a single unit. It is not. It is tense + person, and separating them now saves you relearning it in three lessons' time — the past and future swap out the middle piece and keep the endings.

āḍu-stem + -tunnā-right now + -nuI → āḍutunnānu

Only the middle piece is new today. The person markers are the same six you will use for every tense in the language.

There is no "am"

English needs two words for a continuous tense — am plus playing. Telugu has one. There is no auxiliary verb, so nēnu āḍutunnānu is literally "I playing-am-I", with the "am" baked into the ending.

This is the same zero-copula you met in Lesson 2 with nēnu Josh. Telugu is consistent about it: if English's verb is only there to carry tense and person, Telugu deletes it and suffixes the meaning instead.

The verb goes last, always

Telugu is subject–object–verb. English puts the verb in the middle; Telugu puts it at the end, without exception, and everything else piles up in front of it.

nēnu ṭī tāgutunnānu నేను టీ తాగుతున్నాను — I / tea / am-drinking

Practical consequence: you cannot start speaking until you know how the sentence ends. This is the single hardest habit to build, and it is worth deliberately composing sentences back-to-front — verb first in your head, then the object, then the subject.

Four stem classes decide what the suffix attaches to

The suffix is fixed. What changes verb to verb is the shape the root takes before it. The lesson introduces four patterns without naming them as a set — this is that set. Full list of all 35 verbs is in the next section.

The lesson states this rule wrongly The video says "most verbs end with a vowel, and when we add a suffix, the vowel gets deleted", then gives āḍu as the example. But the u of āḍu plainly survives — the form is āḍutunnānu, not *āḍtunnānu. Deletion happens for n-stems and -kō verbs, not for the u-stems the rule is illustrated with. Go by the forms, which are correct throughout the video, not by the stated rule.

The person marker is a copy of the pronoun's last syllable

This is the good news of the lesson, and it is close to a free ride. Say the pronoun, then end the verb the same way.

nēnuI → āḍutunnānu · nuvvuyou → āḍutunnāvu

It holds for six of the eight pronoun groups. The full table marks the two places it breaks.

Why it works: these endings are eroded pronouns. Telugu once said the equivalent of "play-ing I", and the pronoun fused onto the verb over centuries. You are not learning a coincidence — you are looking at the fossil.

The ṭ quirk: nasals pull the tense marker retroflex

The marker is -tu- for most verbs but -ṭu- after a nasal stem: tiṇṭunnānu, kūrchuṇṭunnānu, uṇṭunnānu. The video calls this "a quirk of pronunciation" and moves on. It is worth ten more seconds: this is assimilation, the same reflex that makes English "in-possible" come out as "impossible".

You do not need to remember which verbs take it. Say the nasal and then a t and your tongue will produce ṭ on its own. It matters for reading, not speaking: when you see తింటున్నాను the retroflex is why the anusvara ం is pronounced ṇ and not n.

This found a bug The Verb Lab was romanizing these as tinṭunnānu and unṭunnānu — dental n where the script says retroflex. 180 forms were affected. Fixed; see the audit.

She and it get a shortened ending

By the pattern, adi should give -tunnadi. In real speech it contracts to -tōndi — āḍutōndi, chēstōndi. Learn the contracted form; the long one sounds bookish.

The reason the video gives is worth keeping: feminine and neuter share one conjugation in Telugu, which makes it by far the most frequently used cell in the table, and high-frequency forms are the ones that erode. You will see the same shortening in the past and future.

Negation is not a suffix on the verb at all

Everything else in Telugu so far has been root plus ending. This is different, and mis-learning it is the commonest error at this stage. To say "I am not playing" you turn the verb into a noun and then say the noun is absent.

āḍa-+ -ḍaṁ→ āḍaḍaṁ “playing / the playing”
āḍaḍaṁ+ lēdu→ āḍaḍaṁ lēdu “playing is not”

The -ḍaṁ form is a verbal noun — "the doing", "the playing" — not an infinitive, though the video calls it one. That distinction is not pedantry: because it is a noun, it takes case endings like any other noun, which is exactly where the purposive chēyaḍāniki "in order to do" comes from. Same -ḍaṁ, put in the dative. You already have that form in the Verb Lab.

lēdu never changes. No person marker, no tense agreement — one word for all eight pronouns. This is the one genuinely easy thing in the lesson.

The confusion that will actually bite you

chēyalēdu is "didn't do" — negative past.
chēyaḍaṁ lēdu is "am not doing" — negative present.

One syllable apart, and both are common. The -ḍaṁ is doing all the work. If you drop it you have changed the tense, not just slurred the word.

Two words running together — the liaison

When a word ending in a vowel meets one starting with a vowel, they fuse in speech: nēnu + ippuḍu → nēnippuḍu నేనిప్పుడు "I now". The video flags this and it is why your wife's Telugu sounds like one long word and the course's sounds like several.

Recognise it, don't force it. It will happen on its own as you get faster. Trying to fake it early produces the wrong vowel.

Postpositions: "in", "to" and "with" come after the noun

Three arrive in this lesson, all of them attaching to the end of the noun rather than sitting in front of it.

SuffixEnglishExample
-lō -లోin and at — Telugu does not distinguish themNew York lō — in New York
Taj Hotel lō — at the Taj
-ki / -ku -కి / -కుtoParis ki — to Paris
-tō -తోwithnā akkatō — with my sister

-ki after a front vowel or i, -ku elsewhere — Paris ki but intiki, nāku. Your ear will settle this faster than a rule will.

Inconsistency in the lesson The video gives Paris ki veḷtunnānu with the suffix, then India veḷtunnānu without it. Both were said by the instructor. Dropping -ki before a well-known place name is normal in fast speech, but including it is never wrong — use it until you have heard enough to drop it naturally.

Four pronouns, one ending

mīru, āyana, āviḍa and vāḷḷu all take -ru. That is a quarter of the pronoun system collapsing into one form — and it is the respectful form, which is the one you should be defaulting to with everyone except children. Learn the -ru row first. With your in-laws and your Hyderabad colleagues it is the only row you need.

Two more things the video says quickly and does not repeat:

Names conjugate like pronouns — and gāru changes the ending

Swap a name in for the pronoun and nothing else changes. But the honorific decides which row you are in:

Rāj pāḍutunnāḍu రాజ్ పాడుతున్నాడు — Raj is singing (a younger man)
Mūrti gāru pani chēstunnāru మూర్తి గారు పని చేస్తున్నారు — Mr Murthy is working

gāru forces the -ru ending. Saying Mūrti gāru chēstunnāḍu mixes a respectful title with a familiar verb — grammatically wrong and socially worse. You met gāru in Lesson 4; this is where it starts affecting the verb.

The non-human rows cover animals, not just objects

Telugu's "neuter" is really non-human. Animals take adi / avi, and so does anything whose gender is unspecified. The video's own example is a robot or an alien speaking Telugu.

avi telugu māṭlāḍutunnāyi అవి తెలుగు మాట్లాడుతున్నాయి — they (animals) are speaking Telugu

Also worth noticing: the feminine and the non-human singular are the same form. āḍutōndi is "she is playing" and "it is playing". The video's framing — "all objects are considered feminine" — is backwards historically; it is more that the feminine merged into the non-human. Either way the practical result is one form to learn instead of two.

Two vocabulary notes the lesson slides past

tiṇḍi తిండి is a small meal or snack; bhōjanaṁ భోజనం is a full meal, usually lunch; ṭiphin టిఫిన్ is breakfast. Three different words where English says "meal" and picks the time of day from context.

tiṇḍi tiṇṭunnānu "I'm eating food" sounds repetitive in romanization but is completely normal Telugu — the noun and the verb share a root the way "sing a song" does.

Cross-check

What this lesson found in the Verb Lab

The Verb Lab's forms were generated from the root-verb PDF before this video existed and had never been checked against an authoritative statement of the paradigm. Video 10 is that statement. All 1,919 generated forms were diffed against it and against tools/te2rom.py.

✓ The paradigm itself is correct

Every stem, every ending, every one of the 35 verbs' present continuous matches what the course teaches. The four stem classes above were derived independently from the PDF and the course confirms all four, including the retroflex marker on nasal stems and the -tōndi contraction. Nothing had to be re-taught.

✗ Fixed — 180 forms romanized with the wrong nasal

Nasal-stem and -kō verbs were rendering as tinṭunnānu, unṭunnānu, kūrchunṭunnānu — dental n before a retroflex ṭ, which this project's convention does not allow and which the Telugu script does not say. Correct: tiṇṭunnānu, uṇṭunnānu, kūrchuṇṭunnānu.

The cause was storing the stem's romanization as a fixed string when the anusvara it represents changes with what follows — the same ం is n before the dental of తింది tindi. Assimilation is now applied at join time, matching te2rom.py. Ten of 35 verbs affected, across every paradigm that uses the non-past stem.

✗ Fixed — the negative present continuous was missing entirely

The Verb Lab had the negative past (chēyalēdu) and the negative habitual (chēyanu) but no chēyaḍaṁ lēdu — the form this lesson spends its last third on. Added as a 15th paradigm, derived from the same stem the purposive already uses, so it needed no new data. The core five is now the core six, and the scope selector reflects that.

Given how close it is to the negative past, having both in one drill is the point rather than a side effect.

✗ Fixed — cc vs chch

Two past stems were stored as vacc- and icc- against the masters' vachch- / ichch-. Twelve forms. The masters win — they are what your Anki cards say.

△ Known and deliberate — two gaps left open

The avi row is not in the Verb Lab. Six persons are drilled; the course teaches eight pronoun groups. The missing one is the non-human plural, the rarest cell in the system — you will read it long before you need to say it. Adding it means a seventh row in every table for one form. Not done; say the word if you want it.

"We" is drilled as manaṁ with the contracted -tunnāṁ, where the course teaches manamu / mēmu with the full -tunnāmu. Both are correct; the Verb Lab shows the colloquial reduction and the course the careful form. Worth knowing that they are the same cell, and that Lesson 1's inclusive/exclusive split still applies — the ending cannot tell you which "we" is meant, only the pronoun can.

Quick self-test

Cover the green line and say each aloud. Questions 9–14 are the ones that matter — production, not recognition.

  • Break āḍutunnāru into its three pieces and name each.āḍu (stem) + tunnā (present continuous) + ru (person: mīru/āyana/āviḍa/vāḷḷu)
  • Why is it tiṇṭunnānu and not tinutunnānu?n-stem: the -u drops, the n fuses to the previous syllable, and the marker goes retroflex after a nasal.
  • Which pronoun does the ending -tunnāvu belong to, and how do you know?nuvvu. The marker copies the pronoun's last syllable.
  • Where does the verb go in a Telugu sentence?Last. Always. Subject–object–verb.
  • Say "she is doing yoga" — and don't use the long form.āme yōgā chēstōndi. (not chēstunnadi)
  • What is the difference between chēyalēdu and chēyaḍaṁ lēdu?chēyalēdu = didn't do (past). chēyaḍaṁ lēdu = am not doing (present).
  • Does lēdu change for "they are not eating"?No. lēdu is the same for all eight pronouns. vāḷḷu tinaḍaṁ lēdu.
  • Your colleague Murthy is older than you. Say "Murthy is working."Mūrti gāru pani chēstunnāru. — gāru forces the -ru ending.
  • Someone asks where you live. Answer, about your real city.nēnu ___ lō uṇṭunnānu.
  • Someone asks where you work. Answer, about your real employer.nēnu ___ lō pani chēstunnānu. — literally "doing work in ___".
  • Say what you are doing at this exact moment.nēnu telugu nērchukuṇṭunnānu.
  • Tell your wife you're speaking with your sister.nēnu nā akkatō māṭlāḍutunnānu. — -tō attaches to akka.
  • Say "I'm not going to India" and then "I'm going to India". Watch the stem.nēnu iṇḍiyā veḷḷaḍaṁ lēdu. / nēnu iṇḍiyā veḷtunnānu. — veḷḷa- in the negative, veḷ- in the positive.
  • Say "I'm having lunch" — and say what's odd about it literally.nēnu bhōjanaṁ chēstunnānu. Literally "I'm doing lunch".
