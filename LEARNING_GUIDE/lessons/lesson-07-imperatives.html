Command verbs & the root form

The first verb lesson, and the hinge of the whole course. A Telugu verb's root form is its informal command — tinu is both "eat" the dictionary entry and "eat!" the order. Everything else in the language is this root plus suffixes, stacked one on the next. Learn the roots now and the rest of the course is addition.

The grammar engine

Structures & rules introduced

Colour code: root verb · politeness suffix · negation suffix. This lesson introduces only two suffixes, but it introduces the mechanism the entire rest of the course runs on.

The root verb is the informal command

In English "eat" the dictionary word and "Eat!" the order look the same by accident. In Telugu it is by design: the root form is the bare command, used to someone close to you or much younger. No suffix, no change.

tinu తిను — "eat" / "eat!"
chēyi చేయి — "do" / "do it!"

This is why the verb list matters so much. Every tense, every mood, every politeness level in the coming lessons is built by attaching something to this form.

-aṇḍi makes it polite — and it is the same suffix you already know

Add -aṇḍi and the command becomes a request. You met this in Lesson 3 on namaskāram aṇḍi and raṇḍi — it is not verb-specific, it is the language's general politeness marker.

tin-eat + -aṇḍipolite → tinaṇḍi

There is no Telugu word for "please". Politeness is a suffix, not an extra word — which is why "please eat" is only an approximation of what tinaṇḍi actually does.

The polite form is the plural form

Speaking to a group and speaking respectfully use the identical form. The logic is the one you already met with mīru in Lesson 1: addressing someone as though they were more than one person is what respect looks like grammatically.

FormWhoExample
tinu తినుOne person, close or much youngeryour child, a close friend
tinaṇḍi తినండిAnyone older, anyone you respect, or any groupyour mother-in-law, a colleague, a room
Your default

Use the -aṇḍi form unless you have a specific reason not to. Getting this wrong is a social error, not a grammatical one — and it is the error a foreigner is most likely to make.

The root's final vowel gets out of the way

Every Telugu root verb ends in a vowel, and Telugu does not allow two vowels to sit next to each other. When -aṇḍi attaches, the root's vowel is deleted or merged. Three patterns cover everything in this lesson.

PatternWhat happensExample
Deletion — roots in -uThe -u dropstinu → tinaṇḍi, tāgu → tāgaṇḍi
not tinuaṇḍi
Merger — roots in -āThe vowels fuserā → raṇḍi
not rāaṇḍi
Absorption — roots in -ōThe ō wins; the suffix shows up as just -ṇḍikūrchō → kūrchōṇḍi, tīsukō → tīsukōṇḍi

One special case: lē ("get up") gains a -v- between the vowels rather than losing either — lē + aṇḍi → lēvaṇḍi. Telugu occasionally inserts a glide instead of deleting.

-vaddu makes it negative, and suffixes stack

Telugu has no "do not". Negation is another suffix, and it goes between the root and the politeness marker. This is the first time you see two suffixes on one verb, and it is the pattern the whole course is built on.

tin-+ -a-vaddu+ -aṇḍi→ tinavaddaṇḍi
VerbDon't — informalDon't — polite
comerāvaddu రావద్దుrāvaddaṇḍi రావద్దండి
dochēyavaddu చేయవద్దుchēyavaddaṇḍi చేయవద్దండి
eattinavaddu తినవద్దుtinavaddaṇḍi తినవద్దండి
drinktāgavaddu తాగవద్దుtāgavaddaṇḍi తాగవద్దండి
tellcheppavaddu చెప్పవద్దుcheppavaddaṇḍi చెప్పవద్దండి
speakmāṭlāḍavaddu మాట్లాడవద్దుmāṭlāḍavaddaṇḍi మాట్లాడవద్దండి

In fast speech the va of -vaddu is barely pronounced after a strong consonant, so tinavaddu lands closer to tinaddu. Recognise it; don't imitate it yet.

This disagrees with the Verb Lab — deliberately

The Verb Lab gives the prohibitive as -ku / -kaṇḍi (chēyaku, chēyakaṇḍi). This lesson teaches -vaddu. Both are real Telugu and they are not identical. chēyaku is a direct prohibition, "don't do it"; chēyavaddu is closer to "it's not wanted / you shouldn't". The course chose -vaddu, which also matches vaddu "no thanks" from Lesson 3. Worth one question to your wife about which she reaches for.

The chēyi hack — borrow any English verb

Attach chēyi to an English word and you have a working Telugu verb. This is not a learner's crutch; native speakers do it constantly, and for many modern concepts there is no commonly used pure-Telugu alternative.

emailany noun + chēyaṇḍido — polite → email chēyaṇḍi

exercise chēyaṇḍi · drive chēyaṇḍi · wait chēyaṇḍi · bill chēyaṇḍi · send chēyaṇḍi · message chēyaṇḍi

The native pattern is the same: pani is "work", so pani chēyaṇḍi is "do the work". When you are stuck for a verb mid-sentence, this is the escape hatch.

Two length contrasts that change the word

Vowel length is meaning-bearing, as it was with nēnu in Lesson 2. Two pairs in today's list are worth isolating now.

ShortLong
paḍu పడు — to fallpāḍu పాడు — to sing
agu — (not a word here)āgu ఆగు — to stop

Also note veḷḷu వెళ్ళు: the ḷ is retroflex — tongue curled back — not the English l. An English l is an acceptable approximation for now, but the two are different sounds and Telugu uses both.

The sequence you will actually hear

raṇḍi, kūrchōṇḍi — "please come in, please have a seat." Then food appears: ēmainā tinaṇḍi ఏమైనా తినండి "have something to eat", ēmainā tāgaṇḍi "have something to drink". ēmainā is "something / anything".

veḷḷu is the standard word and your default. pō is harsher and dialectal. veḷḷipō — literally the two combined — is "go away", and it is the stock line of every Telugu film argument.

Play, sing, love

play — sportāḍuāḍaṇḍiఆడు · ఆడండి
play — musicvāyinchuvāyinchaṇḍiవాయించు · వాయించండి
singpāḍupāḍaṇḍiపాడు · పాడండి
loveprēminchuprēminchaṇḍiప్రేమించు · ప్రేమించండి

Telugu splits "play" into sport (āḍu) and instrument (vāyinchu). prēminchu is built from the Sanskrit noun prēma plus -inchu, the standard way Telugu turns a Sanskrit noun into a verb.

Two flagged forms

parugettaṇḍi and lēvaṇḍi are the two I am least sure of. The captions were badly mangled at both points, and the Verb Lab already flags parugettu as a verb where speakers commonly say parigeḍatānu instead. Check both.

Quick self-test

Cover the green line and say each aloud. Most of these are about choosing the right politeness level, not remembering the word.

  • Your mother-in-law arrives at the door. Say the two things you say next.raṇḍi, kūrchōṇḍi.   (please come in, please have a seat)
  • Offer her something to eat.ēmainā tinaṇḍi.   (ēmainā = something)
  • Tell a colleague to do the work — politely.pani chēyaṇḍi.   (pani chēyi is close friends only at work)
  • Why is tinaṇḍi and not tinuaṇḍi?The root's final -u is deleted before the suffix. Telugu doesn't allow two vowels together.
  • Turn kūrchō polite — and say what happened to the suffix.kūrchōṇḍi.   The ō absorbs the a, so -aṇḍi surfaces as -ṇḍi.
  • Turn rā polite — and say why it isn't rāaṇḍi.raṇḍi.   The two vowels merge rather than both surviving.
  • Tell someone politely not to speak.māṭlāḍavaddaṇḍi.   (root + -vaddu + -aṇḍi, in that order)
  • Don't drink — informally, to a close friend.tāgavaddu.
  • You've forgotten the Telugu for "to park". What do you say?park chēyaṇḍi.   Any English verb + chēyi.
  • Say "please read the newspaper".paper chadavaṇḍi.   paper = newspaper specifically.
  • Which means "sing" — paḍu or pāḍu? What's the other one?pāḍu = sing. paḍu = fall. Vowel length carries the meaning.
  • A traffic policeman wants you to stop. What does he say?āgaṇḍi.   Strangers get the polite form.
  • Say "learn Telugu" (to yourself) and "teach Telugu" (to someone else).Telugu nērchukōṇḍi. / Telugu nērpinchaṇḍi.   -kō is reflexive, -inchu is causative.
  • The film line: tell someone dramatically not to go away.veḷḷipōvaddu.
