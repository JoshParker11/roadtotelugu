One tense for habit and future

nēnu tennis āḍutānu means both "I play tennis" and "I will play tennis." Telugu draws no line between what you do regularly and what you are going to do. This is the deepest structural difference from English in the whole course so far — not a missing word, a missing distinction.

It is also the tense you will use most, because it carries the two sentences every conversation starts with: where you live and what you do for work.

How I processed this

Videos 16 and 19 merged. The captions were the worst of week 2 by a distance: āḍanu came through as "Arduino" and "Adorno", chēstāvu as "Chester" and "Nuevo Chester", chēstāḍu as "Estado de gestion", and māṭlāḍavu as "Malayalam" — repeated eleven times in a row, which is how the final example survives at all.

Two reconstructions I want to flag because they are guesses from context rather than clear audio: "ye daddy" is ēḍādi (year), and "watch" is vachchē (next). Both are confirmed by the compounds around them — prati ēḍādi, vachchē vāraṁ — but the audio itself is gone. Full list at the bottom.

The grammar engine

Structures & rules introduced

Colour code: stem · tense marker · person marker. The positive is a straight substitution into machinery you have twice over. The negative is the new thing, and it is stranger than it looks.

Third tense, same three slots

You now have the pattern well enough that this is almost free.

āḍu-stem + -tā-hab/fut + -nuI → āḍutānu
TenseMarkerāḍuchēyi
Present continuous-tunnā-āḍutunnānuchēstunnānu
Past-ā-āḍānuchēsānu
Habitual & future-tā-āḍutānuchēstānu

And this time the stem rules really do carry over. The video made that claim in Lesson 9 and it was only half true; here it holds completely. chēyi → chēs-, kūrchō → kūrchuṇ-, rā → vas- — identical to the present continuous, because both are built on the same non-past stem.

The negative has no tense marker at all

Every negative so far bolted something on: lēdu for the past, -ḍaṁ lēdu for the present. This one removes instead. Root plus person marker, nothing in between.

āḍ-+ -anu→ āḍanu “I don’t / I won’t play”

Compare the positive āḍu·tā·nu against the negative āḍ·anu. The -tā- is simply gone. That makes these the shortest verb forms in the language, and the fastest to say.

It also means the negative is tenseless in a stronger sense than the positive: with no marker there, nothing but context distinguishes "I don't play" from "I won't play."

A side effect in your favour The negative uses the plain stem, exactly like the negative past. So chēyi stays chēya- and never becomes chēs-; rā stays rā- and never becomes vas-. Every negative in Telugu is built on one stem. Learn that stem and all three negatives fall out of it.

rānu — and the sentence you have already heard

Worth pulling out on its own. rā is suppletive everywhere else — vastānu, vachchānu — but its negative is built on the bare root.

nāku telugu rādu నాకు తెలుగు రాదు — "I don't know Telugu" · literally "Telugu doesn't come to me"

That is this exact paradigm: rā + the she/it negative marker -du. It is the single most useful sentence a Telugu learner owns, and you now know why it is shaped the way it is. Telugu treats knowing a language as something that comes to you.

The she/it negative breaks the mirror rule — and the video explains why

The person marker normally copies the pronoun's last syllable. adi should give -adi. It gives -adu.

āḍ- + -adiexpected → -aduactual

The reason given is a good one: āḍadi would be identical to the pronoun adi sitting right next to it — adi āḍadi. The language shifted the vowel to keep them apart. This is the first time the course explains why an irregularity exists rather than just listing it, and it is the kind of thing worth remembering because it makes the form stick.

An ambiguity the video does not mention The avi negative is -avu — the same ending as nuvvu. So āḍavu is both "you don't play" and "they (things) don't play". Only the pronoun separates them, and Telugu drops pronouns freely. Rare enough not to worry about; worth recognising when it happens.

The two sentences every conversation opens with

This tense carries the existential statements — where you live, where you work. Both are worth memorising with your own details today, because they will come up in the first minute of every conversation you have in Hyderabad.

nēnu Hyderabad lō uṇṭānu నేను Hyderabad లో ఉంటాను — I live in Hyderabad nēnu Acme lō pani chēstānu నేను Acme లో పని చేస్తాను — I work at Acme · literally "I do work in Acme"

uṇḍu covers be / stay / live / exist and the video flags it as a verb you will meet constantly. It is also the subject of Lesson 12. And -lō is both "in" and "at" — Telugu does not separate them.

Telling the time: gaṇṭalaki

gaṇṭalu గంటలు is "hours / o'clock". Add -ki — the same destination marker from iṇṭiki — and it becomes "at".

ēḍu gaṇṭalaki ఏడు గంటలకి — at seven o'clock

The numbers come from Lesson 6, which is now paying off. -ki is doing a third job here: destination (iṇṭiki), recipient, and now a point in time.

The hard part

So how do you tell habit from future?

Two answers, and the first one matters more than the second.

Usually you don't have to

Said with no context, nēnu tennis āḍutānu reads as the habit — "I play tennis". The future reading needs a reason to exist. The ambiguity is far less of a problem in practice than it looks on paper, and the video is explicit that time words are optional.

This is worth internalising rather than fighting. Your instinct will be to disambiguate every sentence. Native speakers don't.

When you do need to, add a time word

Not a different verb form — just a word that makes the timing obvious.

Marks it as a habit

rōju రోజుevery day
prati rōju ప్రతి రోజుevery day prati = every
prati vāraṁ ప్రతి వారంevery week

vachchē is rā in disguise

"Next week" in Telugu is literally "the coming week" — vachchē is a form of vas-, the same stem behind vastānu and vachchānu. English does exactly this with "the coming week" too; Telugu just made it the default.

You have not formally met this form yet. It is the relative participle — a verb turned into an adjective — which the verb forms deep dive lists as the big thing waiting beyond the Verb Lab. This is your first sighting of it.

Week 2's grammar, complete

The whole tense system on one verb

With Lessons 8, 9 and 10 done, this is everything you can say about an action — six cells, and they cover the overwhelming majority of ordinary conversation. Drill this block for every verb you care about.

TensePositiveTeluguNegativeTelugu
Habitual & futureI play / I will playāḍutānuఆడుతానుāḍanuఆడను
Present continuousI am playingāḍutunnānuఆడుతున్నానుāḍaḍaṁ lēduఆడడం లేదు
PastI playedāḍānuఆడానుāḍalēduఆడలేదు
All three negatives begin āḍa-

āḍanu · āḍaḍaṁ lēdu · āḍalēdu. Three different meanings separated by what comes after the stem, and nothing else. This is the highest-confusion point in the language so far and the one place where slowing down to be precise is worth it.

The distinguishing move: lēdu anywhere means it is not happening now or did not happen; no lēdu at all means doesn't / won't. Then -ḍaṁ before lēdu is the present, bare stem before it is the past.

What week 2 actually bought you

Three tenses, two polarities, eight pronouns. But the count that matters is smaller: you learned three tense markers — -tunnā-, -ā-, -tā- — and reused one set of person markers across all of them. That is why the third lesson was easier than the first.

  • Say where you live and where you work.nēnu ___ lō uṇṭānu. / nēnu ___ lō pani chēstānu. — and note -lō covers both "in" and "at".
  • Give all three negatives of āḍu and what each means.āḍanu (don't/won't) · āḍaḍaṁ lēdu (am not playing) · āḍalēdu (didn't play)
  • Which stem do all three negatives use?The plain negative stem — āḍa-, chēya-, rā-. Never the s-stem or the past stem. This is the one thing that makes negatives easy.
  • "I go to the temple every week."nēnu prati vāraṁ guḍiki veḷtānu.
  • Now next week.nēnu vachchē vāraṁ guḍiki veḷtānu. — vachchē is literally "coming".
  • "I eat breakfast at seven every day."nēnu rōju ēḍu gaṇṭalaki ṭiphin tiṇṭānu.
  • Why does nāku telugu rādu mean "I don't know Telugu"?It's this paradigm: rā + the she/it negative -du. Literally "Telugu doesn't come to me."
  • Murthy doesn't work in India — respectfully.Mūrti gāru India lō pani chēyaru. — gāru forces -ru, and the stem drops back to chēya-.
  • Say the same three sentences about your day twice: once as yesterday, once as every day....-ānu for yesterday, ...-tānu for every day. Only the tense marker moves.
