Greetings, politeness & the three ways to say no

A phrasebook lesson with two real grammar payloads underneath it. First: aṇḍi is a productive politeness suffix you can attach almost anywhere — Telugu encodes courtesy in endings, not in words like "please." Second: English's single "no" splits into three Telugu words that are not interchangeable.

How I processed this

The roughest captions yet — this transcript rendered avunu as "avenue," lēdu as "ladoo," vaddu as "what do," dayacēsi as "the case," parvālēdu as "part," enta as "Eddie," and bāgunnānu as "Bhagwan Nanu." I reconstructed each from the surrounding explanation and normalized to the site's diacritics.

Two to confirm with your wife: whether manchidi is really what she'd use to describe a person (see rule 09), and the spelling she prefers for śabhāṣ, which has several written variants.

The grammar engine

Structures & rules introduced

Same colour code: base word · meaning unit · politeness / suffix. Most of this lesson is vocabulary, but rules 02–04 and 06–08 are structural and will keep paying off.

namaskāraṁ — the greeting and the gesture

The standard Telugu greeting, equivalent to Hindi namaste. The lesson gives its sense as an acknowledgement of the divinity in the person you're addressing — it is a respectful greeting, not a casual "hi."

It comes with a physical form: rather than waving, you bring your palms together with fingers pointing upward. In a Hyderabad workplace this pairing matters — the gesture without the word, or the word without the gesture, both read as slightly incomplete.

namaskāraṁ నమస్కారం — hello / greetings

aṇḍi attaches to almost anything

This is the load-bearing rule of the lesson. aṇḍi అండి is a politeness suffix, and you can append it to greetings, answers, and whole phrases to raise the register. You met it in Lesson 1 as a respectful address particle; here it becomes a productive tool.

WORDany greeting / answer + aṇḍipoliteness → respectful version
namaskāraṁ → namaskāram aṇḍi — more respectful greeting
avunu → avunandi అవునండి — "yes" → polite "yes"
lēdu → lēdandi  ·  vaddu → vaddandi  ·  kādu → kādandi

Given your goal — respectful conversation with colleagues and senior leaders — this single suffix is probably the highest-leverage thing in the lesson. When in doubt, add it.

Telugu politeness lives in suffixes, not in words

There is a word for "please" — dayacēsi దయచేసి — but the lesson is explicit that it's rarely used in conversation. The politeness is already carried by the ending, so adding "please" on top is redundant.

"Please come in" is just raṇḍi. You do not say dayacēsi raṇḍi. This is a genuine structural difference from English, where politeness is mostly lexical ("please," "would you mind," "sorry to bother you").

Transfer error to avoid English speakers reach for "please" constantly. In Telugu that instinct should be redirected into choosing the -aṇḍi form of the verb. Politeness is a grammatical choice here, not an added word.

raṇḍi = rā + aṇḍi

Worth pulling apart, because it shows the suffix fusing rather than just being appended. rā రా means "come"; add the respectful suffix and you get raṇḍi రండి, "please come in."

rācome + aṇḍirespectful → raṇḍi

Colloquially raṇḍi is how you actually welcome someone. The formal word for "welcome," svāgataṁ స్వాగతం, exists but the lesson notes it's rare in conversation — you'll see it on banners and hear it in speeches more than in doorways.

Keep an eye on rā. It comes back in rule 11 doing something completely different.

Goodbye is "I'll come back"

Telugu has no direct equivalent of "goodbye." The word used is vastānu వస్తాను, which literally means I'll come back. The lesson frames this as a cultural preference: announcing a return is warmer than announcing a departure.

vastānu వస్తాను — "goodbye" (lit. I'll come back)
vastānandi వస్తానండి — the polite version, with aṇḍi

This is the kind of detail worth knowing before you use it with in-laws or senior colleagues — saying the literal "I'm leaving" equivalent would land as blunter than you intend.

Thanks: formal Telugu vs. the colloquial hybrid

dhanyavādālu ధన్యవాదాలు (which you already have from Lesson 2) is used exclusively in formal situations — lectures, courses, written notes. Using it for everyday thanks sounds stilted.

What people increasingly say instead is a Telugu–English hybrid: thanks aṇḍi. The English word carries the meaning, the Telugu suffix carries the respect.

dhanyavādālu — formal, ceremonial
thanks aṇḍi — everyday, respectful, extremely common

This is the same loanword pattern from Lesson 2's professions, now applied to a social formula. Code-mixing here isn't sloppy — it's the current colloquial norm, and it's exactly what you'll hear in Hyderabad.

Three different words for "no"

The biggest concept in the lesson. English "no" covers three distinct jobs that Telugu keeps separate. Picking the wrong one isn't a small error — it changes what you're denying.

WordTeluguDeniesRoughly
lēduలేదుExistence / presence"There isn't / it's not there / I don't have it"
vadduవద్దుDesire / wanting"I don't want it / no thanks"
kāduకాదుIdentity / a statement"It is not / that's not right"

All three take the politeness suffix the same way: lēdandi, vaddandi, kādandi.

Notice that kādu is the negation of the identity sentences you built in Lesson 1. nēnu vidyārthini ("I am a student") is denied with kādu, not with the other two — this is your first negation tool for the frame you already know.

idi kāru kādu. — "This is not a car." (denying the statement)
vaddu, thanks aṇḍi. — "No thanks." (declining an offer)

Yes is simple — and takes the suffix too

avunu అవును is "yes." The polite form is avunandi అవునండి. Nothing complicated here, but note the pattern holding: base form for peers and intimates, -aṇḍi form for everyone else.

Also useful and register-neutral: sarē సరే, "okay" — the general-purpose agreement word.

Two different "good"s

Another English word that splits in Telugu, and the distinction is subtle rather than sharp.

WordTeluguSenseUse for
manchidiమంచిదిNice, noble, morally goodCharacter, worthiness
bāgundiబాగుందిFine, positive, going wellFood, trips, experiences, states
"How was the food?" → bāgundi. — "It's good / it was fine."
Verify this one The lesson says you'd describe a good person as manchidi. That form is neuter, and for a person you'd more often hear manchi manishi మంచి మనిషి ("a good person") or a gendered form. Ask your wife what she'd actually say about a colleague — the underlying point (manchi = moral good, bāgu = things going well) holds either way.

"How are you" is literally "are you fine?"

bāgunnārā బాగున్నారా — built on the same bāgu root as bāgundi. You can front it with the pronoun, but you don't have to.

bāgunnārā? బాగున్నారా?  ·  mīru bāgunnārā? — "How are you?" (mīru is optional)
bāgunnānu. బాగున్నాను.  ·  nēnu bāgunnānu. — "I'm fine."

Two things to notice. First, the pronoun is optional — the ending already tells you who's involved, so Telugu drops what English requires. Second, look at those endings: -nu for "I" and -rā marking the question to "you." That's the same person-marking logic from Lesson 1 showing up on a different kind of word.

Note that mīru is the respectful "you" — this is the version you'd use with colleagues and elders, and the one worth drilling first.

"I don't know Telugu" = "Telugu doesn't come"

A framing you'd never guess from English. rādu రాదు is the negative of rā ("come") — so the sentence literally says Telugu doesn't come [to me].

Telugu rādu. తెలుగు రాదు. — "I don't know Telugu."

Languages, in Telugu, are things that come to you or don't. That's also why this phrase is genuinely useful for you right now — it's the honest, natural way to signal your level rather than apologizing in English.

The question counterpart uses a different root: telusā తెలుసా, "do you know?"

English telusā? ఇంగ్లీష్ తెలుసా? — "Do you know English?"

Practical frames: enta and idigō

enta ఎంత means "how much." Combine it with idi — the near demonstrative from Lesson 1 — and you have a complete transaction question with no verb required.

idithis entahow much → idi enta?
idi enta? ఇది ఎంత? — "How much is this?"
idigō ఇదిగో  ·  idigōṇḍi — "here you go," handing something over

Notice idi enta is another zero-copula sentence — the same Lesson 1 rule, now doing question work. And idigō is transparently idi + an emphatic ending: "here's this one."

Reference

Choosing the right "no"

The one thing from this lesson most likely to trip you up mid-conversation. Ask yourself: am I denying that something exists, that I want it, or that a statement is true?

SituationUseWhy
lēdu — denying existence or presence
"Is there any coffee?"lēdu లేదుThere isn't any — it doesn't exist right now
"Do you have a pen?"lēduNot in my possession
"Is Ravi here?"lēdandiHe's not present — polite form
vaddu — declining, refusing, not wanting
"More tea?"vaddu వద్దుI don't want it
Declining an offer politelyvaddandiSame, with respect — this is the workplace-safe form
kādu — negating a statement or identity
"You're a teacher, right?"kādu కాదుThat's not what I am — negates the identity sentence
"Is this your car?"kādandiIt is not — polite correction
The connection back to Lesson 1

kādu is the negation of the zero-copula identity frame. You built Rāj vidyārthi ("Raj is a student") in Lesson 1 with no verb at all; kādu is what you add to deny it. That means you now have both halves of the most basic sentence type in the language.

Quick self-test

Cover the green line and say each aloud first. Several of these are about picking the right word, not just any word.

  • Greet someone respectfully — and what do your hands do?namaskāram aṇḍi.   (palms together, fingers pointing up — not a wave)
  • Someone offers you more food. You've had enough.vaddandi.   (declining = vaddu, not lēdu)
  • Someone asks if there's any coffee left. There isn't.lēdandi.   (existence = lēdu)
  • Someone assumes you're a teacher. You're not.kādandi.   (negating an identity statement = kādu)
  • "How are you?" — ask it respectfully, then answer.mīru bāgunnārā? / nēnu bāgunnānu.   (both pronouns optional)
  • Break down raṇḍi into its pieces.rā (come) + aṇḍi (respectful) → raṇḍi.   No separate "please" needed.
  • Say goodbye politely — and what does it literally mean?vastānandi.   Literally "I'll come back."
  • Tell someone you don't know Telugu.Telugu rādu.   (lit. "Telugu doesn't come")
  • Ask the price of something in front of you.idi enta?   (idi from Lesson 1 + enta, no verb)
  • Thank a colleague in the way people actually do.thanks aṇḍi.   (dhanyavādālu is formal/ceremonial only)
