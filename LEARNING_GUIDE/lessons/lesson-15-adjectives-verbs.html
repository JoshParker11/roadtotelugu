Two slots for a description, and 19 new verbs

Lesson 12 gave you -gā: andangā undi, "it is beautiful". That form cannot go in front of a noun. For that you need a different suffix, and this list is where it turns up.

The 50-verb list is mostly verbs you already have. The 19 that are new are now in the Verb Lab, which is up to 54 verbs and 3,282 drillable forms.

How I processed this

Written source again, so the same class of damage: nērpinchu arrived as ేర్పించు and lekkapeṭṭu as ెక్కపెట్టు — first consonant dropped, orphan vowel sign left behind. Repaired.

Romanization re-derived from script throughout, correcting five spellings. The one that mattered: "torn" is listed as cimpa but the script says chimpina — a different word, and a more interesting one.

Four entries in the adjective list are not adjectives, or not the form given. Those are below; the Anki list has them corrected.

The grammar

Two suffixes, two slots

English uses one word in both places — "the flower is beautiful", "a beautiful flower". Telugu uses two different forms, and they are not interchangeable.

SlotSuffixExampleScript
After the nounpredicate — "it IS beautiful"-gāpuvvu andangā undiపువ్వు అందంగా ఉంది
Before the nounattributive — "a beautiful flower"-ainaandamaina puvvuఅందమైన పువ్వు

Both come off the same noun, andaṁ ("beauty"). Neither is the adjective — the noun is. -gā sends it to the predicate, -aina sends it in front of a noun. Miss the distinction and *andangā puvvu comes out, which is not a phrase.

Some adjectives need no suffix at all

A dozen of these are bare stems that sit in front of a noun as they are — no -aina required.

pedda illu పెద్ద ఇల్లు — a big house kotta kāru కొత్త కారు — a new car

The bare set: pedda, chinna, kotta, manchi, gaṭṭi, sanna, tīpi, vēḍi, kāraṁ, muriki, chavaka, pēda, cheḍu. Learn which class a word is in when you learn the word — there is no rule that predicts it.

-ani is a third ending doing the same job

"Cold" is challani before a noun, but you already met challagā in the predicate. Same word, same two slots, a different attributive ending.

challa+-gā→challagā undi it is cold
challa+-ani→challani nīḷḷu cold water

Also pullani (sour). -aina is much the commoner of the two; treat -ani as a small closed set to recognise.

Where the nouns from Lesson 14 pay off

You now have both halves of an enormous number of true sentences:

ī kāphī chālā vēḍigā undi ఈ కాఫీ చాలా వేడిగా ఉంది — this coffee is very hot nāku rucikaramaina bhōjanaṁ kāvāli నాకు రుచికరమైన భోజనం కావాలి — I want a delicious meal

The second is worth memorising as a frame: nāku + [adjective]aina + [noun] + kāvāli. That is a complete request, and it recombines with everything in Lesson 14.

Corrected before import

Four entries that are not what the list says

Worth reading rather than skipping — each one is a grammar point wearing a mistake.

✗ buddhimantuḍu and dhanavantuḍu are nouns, not adjectives

Listed as "clever" and "rich". They are masculine nouns — "a clever man", "a rich man". The -uḍu ending is the masculine agent marker you have seen on vāḍu and every -āḍu verb ending.

Applied to a woman they are simply wrong; the feminine is -urālu — dhanavanturālu. To use them as actual adjectives you want telivaina and dhanavantamaina.

✗ pullanivi is plural

Listed as "sour". It is pullani + -vi, the neuter plural — "they are sour". The adjective is pullani. Corrected in the list below.

△ chimpina ("torn") is a verb in disguise

The source romanizes it cimpa, but the script says chimpina — and that is not a basic adjective at all. It is chimpu ("tear") turned into one.

This is the relative participle, the big structure the verb forms deep dive flags as waiting past the Verb Lab: Telugu has no "which/that" clauses, so it makes the verb into an adjective instead. chimpina chokkā is "a torn shirt", literally "a tore shirt". Third sighting this month after vachchē and ani. Still filed, not taught.

△ Two pairs are the same word listed twice

telivaina appears as both "intelligent" and "smart"; poḍavaina as both "long" and "high". For height specifically you want etthaina (from ettu, height) — poḍavaina is length. And manchidi ("good") is manchi + di, so it means "a good one" and cannot go in front of a noun; the attributive is plain manchi.

Verb Lab

19 new verbs, 54 total

Most of the 50-verb list you already had — cheyyi, vrāyi, naḍuvu and visuru are spelling variants of verbs already in the Lab, not new entries. Nineteen are genuinely new and are now generated across every paradigm.

Three that are worth more than the other sixteen

paḍu "fall". The minimal pair of pāḍu "sing", which you already have. paḍānu "I fell" against pāḍānu "I sang" — one vowel. Already seeded in review/minimal-pairs.tsv; this is the day it becomes drillable in both directions.

vāḍu "use". Spelled and pronounced identically to vāḍu "he". Only position separates them — a pronoun opens the sentence, a verb closes it. vāḍu vāḍutāḍu is a real sentence: "he will use it".

tinipinchu "feed". The causative of tinu "eat" — literally "cause to eat". Put them side by side and the -inchu causative stops being a class label and becomes obvious: what you do versus what you make someone else do. Same relationship as nērchukō / nērpinchu from Lesson 7.

△ Six of the nineteen are flagged

ēḍuvu, aruvu, neṭṭu, kālchu, modaleṭṭu, lekkapeṭṭu. No source lists their stems — I derived them from the class patterns the audited verbs establish, which is reliable for the regular classes and a guess for these.

Specifically: neṭṭu and the two peṭṭu compounds are assumed to soften ṭṭ → ḍ in the non-past the way koṭṭu → koḍatānu does, giving neḍatānu and modaleḍatānu. That is the pattern, but neṭṭutānu is also heard. They carry conf:'check' and surface in the Verb Lab's review page.

One change to an existing entry

"Dance" was stored as nāṭyaṁ chēyi. The course teaches nṛtyaṁ chēyi, which is the general word — nāṭyaṁ is classical dance specifically. Updated.

Quick self-test

  • "The flower is beautiful" and "a beautiful flower".puvvu andangā undi. / andamaina puvvu. — -gā for the predicate, -aina before a noun.
  • Why is *andangā puvvu wrong?-gā only works after the noun. It's not the attributive form.
  • "A big house" — does pedda need a suffix?No. pedda illu. A dozen adjectives are bare stems; there's no rule, learn the class with the word.
  • Say "cold water" and "the water is cold".challani nīḷḷu. / nīḷḷu challagā unnāyi. — -ani attributive, -gā predicate, and nīḷḷu is plural.
  • Why can't you call your wife dhanavantuḍu?It's a masculine noun — "a rich man". -uḍu is the masculine agent ending; the feminine is dhanavanturālu.
  • "I want a delicious meal."nāku rucikaramaina bhōjanaṁ kāvāli.
  • Difference between paḍānu and pāḍānu?I fell / I sang. One vowel.
  • Translate vāḍu vāḍutāḍu."He will use it." Same word twice — pronoun first, verb last.
  • What is tinipinchu made of?tinu "eat" + the -inchu causative: cause to eat, i.e. feed.
  • Describe five things around you, once before the noun and once after.[adj]aina [noun] / [noun] [adj]gā undi. Watch which adjectives are bare.
