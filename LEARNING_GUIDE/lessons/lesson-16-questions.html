Questions — one vowel, then one letter

Any Telugu statement becomes a yes/no question by adding one vowel to the end. And every question word starts with e-, has a near answer starting i- and a far answer starting a-.

That second pattern is the most elegant thing in the course. English has a broken fragment of it — where / here / there, this / that. Telugu has it complete, across seven question words, with no exceptions.

How I processed this

Opens with "Anarchy." again. The question words come through as "yella", "EMT", "Jakarta", "Hebrew", "yavru", "Yentha", "Yeni" — recoverable. The correlative pairs at the end collapse almost entirely (inta/anta as "into or onto", inni/anni as "any or any"), so those were rebuilt from the pattern rather than from the audio. The pattern is exceptionless, which is what makes that safe.

Every form here is checked against tools/te2rom.py.

The easy way

Add -ā and you have a question

No word order change, no auxiliary, no rising intonation required. Any statement, any length.

mīru bāgunnāru+-ā →mīru bāgunnārā?

Drop the pronoun and you have the greeting

The verb already carries the person, so mīru is optional. bāgunnārā? on its own is "how are you?" respectfully, and bāgunnāvā? is the casual version. This is very likely the first Telugu anyone will say to you.

Note what it literally asks: not "how are you" but "are you well?" — a yes/no question. The answer is bāgunnānu, "I am well".

The vowel replaces, it doesn't just append -tundi → -tundā, not *-tundiā. Telugu will not put two vowels together, the rule you met back in Lesson 7. The final vowel of the verb gives way to the ā.

The question word goes immediately before the verb

Not at the front, as in English. The verb stays last — as it always has — and the question word slots in right in front of it.

mīru elā unnāru? మీరు ఎలా ఉన్నారు? — how are you? chārminār ekkaḍa undi? చార్మినార్ ఎక్కడ ఉంది? — where is the Charminar?

Said at speed the vowels run together: mīrelā unnāru, nuvvelā unnāvu. The same liaison as nēnippuḍu from Lesson 8.

ēmiṭi or ēmi — decided by whether there is a verb

No verb → ēmiṭi, at the endVerb → ēmi, just before it
mī pēru ēmiṭi?
మీ పేరు ఏమిటి?what is your name?		 mīru ēmi chēstāru?
మీరు ఏమి చేస్తారు?what do you do?

Both are pronounced ēṇṭi as often as ēmiṭi — the same word, and the course says so explicitly. Fast: mī pērēmiṭi?

One question, five different verbs

"How is your ___?" is the same question word every time. What changes is the agreement, and this set is the clearest drill of Lesson 13's table you will get.

Teluguwhy that ending
mī nānna elā unnāru? మీ నాన్నyour father-āru — respect
mī amma elā unnāru? మీ అమ్మyour mother-āru — respect
mī pillalu elā unnāru? మీ పిల్లలుyour kids-āru — plural
mī koḍuku elā unnāḍu? మీ కొడుకుyour son-āḍu — masculine
mī kūturu elā undi? మీ కూతురుyour daughterundi — feminine
mī kotta illu elā undi? మీ కొత్త ఇల్లుyour new houseundi — neuter
Parents get the respectful ending, children do not nānna and amma are singular people but take -āru. Your son takes -āḍu. That is not grammar, it is respect encoded as plural — the same logic as mīru, and getting it backwards is a social error, not a grammatical one.

Telugu has no "where are you from?"

You ask where you come from, in the habitual — which is what the habitual tense is for.

mīru ekkaḍa nunchi vastāru? మీరు ఎక్కడ నుంచి వస్తారు? — where are you from?

Swap to the present continuous and it becomes a different question: mīru ekkaḍa nunchi vastunnāru? is "where are you coming from right now". One morpheme apart, and you will be asked both.

enta and enni — Telugu makes you choose

English says "how much" and "how many" too, but is lazy about it. Telugu is not.

idi enta? ఇది ఎంత? — how much is this? (shopping) enni paṇḍlu unnāyi? ఎన్ని పండ్లు ఉన్నాయి? — how many fruit are there?

Note paṇḍlu, the plural of paṇḍu from Lesson 14 — and unnāyi, the neuter-plural verb.

Age uses enta, not ēmiṭi. Telugu asks how much is your age: mī vayasu enta? మీ వయసు ఎంత?

The pattern worth the whole lesson

e- asks · i- is near · a- is far

Every question word has two matched answers. Change the first letter and you have them. No exceptions.

You already know four of these fourteen

ikkaḍa, ippuḍu, idi and adi are all in your deck already, learned as unrelated vocabulary. They are one system, and the other ten come free.

English has fragments of this — where / here / there, this / that, and the archaic thus. Telugu kept the whole thing, which means inta "this much", anni "that many", anduku "that's why" are all derivable rather than memorised.

And the two systems combine. ēdi bāgundi? asks "which is good?" — but to ask "this one?" you take the answer word and add the -ā particle: idā? adā? ఇదా? అదా?

Quick self-test

  • Turn mīru telugu māṭlāḍutāru into a question.mīru telugu māṭlāḍutārā? — just add -ā.
  • Greet someone respectfully, then casually, with no pronoun.bāgunnārā? / bāgunnāvā? — the verb already carries the person.
  • Why is it māṭlāḍutundā and not *māṭlāḍutundiā?Telugu won't put two vowels together. The final i gives way to the ā.
  • "What is your name?" and "What are you doing?" — and why different words for "what"?mī pēru ēmiṭi? / mīru ēmi chēstunnāru? — ēmiṭi with no verb, ēmi before one.
  • Where does a question word go?Immediately before the verb. The verb still comes last.
  • "How is your daughter?" and "How is your son?"mī kūturu elā undi? / mī koḍuku elā unnāḍu? — feminine takes undi, masculine -āḍu.
  • Why does "how is your father" use -āru?Respect encoded as plural, same as mīru. Children get the singular.
  • Ask someone where they're from.mīru ekkaḍa nunchi vastāru? — habitual. The continuous vastunnāru? asks where they're coming from right now.
  • Fill in: elā / ikkaḍa / ___ / eppuḍu / ippuḍu / ___ilā-alā, ekkaḍa, akkaḍa, appuḍu. e- asks, i- is near, a- is far.
  • You know enni. What are "this many" and "that many"?inni and anni. Swap the first letter.
  • "It rained, that's why the train is late."varṣaṁ paḍindi, anduku ṭrain ālasyaṁ. — anduku is the far answer to enduku.
  • How old are you? Careful which question word.mī vayasu enta? — how much, not what.
  • Ask five real questions about the room you're in.Question word immediately before the verb, verb last.
