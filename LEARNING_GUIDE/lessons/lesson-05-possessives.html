Possessive pronouns

Every pronoun from Lesson 1 has a matching possessive form, and the whole set follows three behaviours: unchanged, final vowel shifts, or completely different word. Once you know which pronoun does which, you get twelve possessives out of pronouns you already own.

How I processed this

Captions were rough on the third-person forms — atani came through as "attorney," dāni as "Danny," vāṭi as "vaati," and pērlu as "Perilloux." Reconstructed from the surrounding explanation and normalised to the site's diacritics.

The word for "what" is written ēmiṭi ఏమిటి but the transcript's pronunciation is the colloquial ēnti ఏంటి. Both are given below; ask your wife which she uses in Hyderabad.

This lesson sharpens Lesson 2

Lesson 2 taught mā as simply "our." This lesson reveals it's more precise than that: mā is the exclusive "our" and mana is the inclusive one — exactly mirroring the mēmu/manamu split from Lesson 1. See rule 03.

The grammar engine

Structures & rules introduced

Colour code: possessor · possessed noun · changed ending. This lesson is one system with three sub-patterns — rules 05–08 are the whole engine.

Every pronoun has a possessive partner

Telugu doesn't build possession with a separate word like English of, or with an ending like 's. Each pronoun simply has a second form that means "belonging to that person," and it sits directly in front of the noun.

POSSESSIVEwhose NOUNwhat → complete phrase

You already met two of these in Lesson 2: nā ("my") and mā ("our"). This lesson completes the set and shows the pattern behind it.

nā pēru నా పేరు — "my name"

Still no apostrophe-s

Worth restating because this lesson stacks a lot of possessives: the possessive form is the only marker. Nothing attaches to the noun that follows, and nothing joins them.

atani pēru అతని పేరు — "his name" — two words, no connector

This is the same rule from Lesson 2 that gave you nā ḍākṭar pēru ("my doctor's name"). Possession is expressed by adjacency, all the way down.

"Our" splits inclusive vs. exclusive — just like "we"

Lesson 1 gave you two words for "we": manamu (including the listener) and mēmu (excluding them). Their possessives split the same way.

PronounPossessiveMeans
manamu మనముmana మనour — including you, the listener
mēmu మేముmā మాour — not including you
This explains a Lesson 2 detail Lesson 2 told you to say mā amma for "my mother." Now you can see why it's mā and not mana — you're excluding your listener, because they aren't part of your family. Talking to your own sibling, mana amma would be the natural choice. I'd verify that specific switch with your wife, but the logic follows directly from the inclusive/exclusive rule.

"Your" splits by register: mī vs nī

The same formal/informal choice you learned for mīru and nuvvu in Lesson 1, carried into the possessive.

PronounPossessiveUse with
mīru మీరు formalmī మీElders, strangers, colleagues, groups
nuvvu నువ్వు informalnī నీClose friends, people younger than you
mī pēru మీ పేరు — "your name" (respectful)
nī pēru నీ పేరు — "your name" (familiar)

Note how short these are. The pronouns mīru/nuvvu shrink to a single syllable as possessives — easy to miss in fast speech, so worth training your ear on.

Pattern A — formal forms don't change at all

The three respectful third-person pronouns are identical as possessives. No transformation to learn.

PronounPossessive
āyana ఆయన he, formalāyana ఆయన hisunchanged
āviḍa ఆవిడ she, formalāviḍa ఆవిడ herunchanged
āme ఆమె she, neutralāme ఆమె herunchanged

Note that āme is neutral rather than formal but still doesn't change — the "unchanged" group isn't strictly the formal register.

āyana pēru ఆయన పేరు — "his name" (respectful)

Pattern B — the final vowel shifts

The masculine neutral and informal pronouns end in -u, and that vowel becomes -i.

atanuhe, neutral → atanihis
PronounPossessive
atanu అతను he, neutralatani అతని hisu → i
vāḍu వాడు he, informalvāḍi వాడి hisu → i
vāḷḷu వాళ్ళు they, peoplevāḷḷa వాళ్ళ theiru → a

You've seen this -u → -i shift before. Lesson 2's rule 04 did exactly the same thing when attaching the person marker: bhāratīyuḍu → bhāratīyuḍini. Telugu keeps reusing it, so it's worth treating as a general habit of the language rather than a one-off.

Watch out for vāḷḷu → vāḷḷa — it shifts to -a, not -i. That's the one exception in this group.

Pattern C — the word changes completely

Two possessives are not derivable from their pronouns at all. These simply have to be memorised.

PronounPossessive
adi అది she informal / itdāni దాని her / itsnew form
avi అవి they, objectsvāṭi వాటి theirnew form

Both are the non-human / neuter forms, which is a useful memory hook: the object pronouns are the irregular ones. Since adi doubles as the informal "she," dāni covers both "its" and informal "her."

Hear this pair in a song: Saranga Dariya dāni pēru దాని పేరు — "her name" (informal) or "its name"
vāṭi pērlu వాటి పేర్లు — "their names" (objects)

Near forms transform the same way

Lesson 1 gave you the near/far pairs — atanu/itanu, adi/idi, and so on. The possessives follow suit, so you don't learn a second system.

itanu → itani — "this man's"
idi → dīni — "this one's" · mirrors adi → dāni
vīḷḷu → vīḷḷa — "these people's"
Worth confirming The lesson demonstrates the far forms in detail and states that the others follow the same pattern. I've extended it to the near forms above by applying the rule, which is how the language works — but these specific near-possessives weren't spelled out on screen. Quick sanity check with your wife before drilling them.

Plural possession: pēru → pērlu

When several people each have a name, the noun pluralises with the -lu ending you learned in Lesson 1.

pēruname + -luplural → pērlu
vāḷḷa pērlu వాళ్ళ పేర్లు — "their names"
mā pērlu — "our names" (not including you)
mana pērlu — "our names" (including you)

ēmiṭi — "what," and the liaison it triggers

ēmiṭi ఏమిటి means "what." Colloquially you'll hear ēnti ఏంటి. Asking someone's name needs no verb — just possessive, noun, question word.

mī pēru ēmiṭi? మీ పేరు ఏమిటి? — "What is your name?" (respectful)

But pēru ends in a vowel and ēmiṭi starts with one — which triggers the fast-speech vowel deletion from Lesson 2's rule 03. At conversational speed the two words fuse:

pēruname + ēntiwhat → pēreṇṭi
mī pēreṇṭi? — what you'll actually hear

This is the second time the same sound rule has shown up. If a Telugu phrase sounds like one long word, assume a vowel got deleted at a boundary and try separating it.

With strangers, always default to mī

The lesson makes this explicit, and it matters for you specifically. If you're asking someone's name at all, you probably don't know them — which means the respectful form is the right call.

mī pēru ēmiṭi? — safe with anyone
nī pēru ēnti? — only for close friends or someone clearly younger
The pattern across three lessons mīru over nuvvu, mī over nī, -aṇḍi on your answers, gāru after names. Telugu gives you a respect choice at almost every turn, and for workplace use the answer is consistently the same one. When in doubt, take the formal option.

The shortcut worth internalising

Only four forms are genuinely unpredictable: nā, dāni, vāṭi, and the mana/mā pair. Everything else is either identical to the pronoun or a single vowel shifting. Learn those four properly and the other eight fall out of rules you already know.

Put it together

Introducing several people

The lesson closes with a short piece where a speaker introduces friends and family, cycling through every third-person possessive. Here's the same shape built from scratch, so you can see each form doing its job in context.

నమస్కారం. నా పేరు రవి.namaskāraṁ. nā pēru Ravi.
Hello. My name is Ravi.
ఆయన మా నాన్న. ఆయన పేరు మూర్తి.āyana mā nāna. āyana pēru Murthy.
That is my father. His name is Murthy. (formal — unchanged)
ఆవిడ మా అమ్మ. ఆవిడ పేరు సునీత.āviḍa mā amma. āviḍa pēru Sunita.
That is my mother. Her name is Sunita. (formal — unchanged)
ఆమె మా అక్క. ఆమె పేరు దివ్య.āme mā akka. āme pēru Divya.
She is my elder sister. Her name is Divya. (neutral — unchanged)
అతను మా చిన్నాన్న. అతని పేరు సంజయ్.atanu mā chinnāna. atani pēru Sanjay.
He is my father's younger brother. His name is Sanjay. (neutral — u→i)
వాడు నా ఫ్రెండ్. వాడి పేరు పీటర్.vāḍu nā friend. vāḍi pēru Peter.
He is my friend. His name is Peter. (informal — u→i)
అది నా క్లాస్‌మేట్. దాని పేరు గీత.adi nā classmate. dāni pēru Gita.
That's my classmate. Her name is Gita. (informal — new form)
వాళ్ళ పేర్లు పీటర్, గీత.vāḷḷa pērlu Peter, Gita.
Their names are Peter and Gita. (plural — u→a, noun takes -lu)
What this demonstrates

Every sentence is zero-copula — no "is" anywhere. The kinship terms come from Lesson 4, mā for family from Lesson 2, the pronouns from Lesson 1, and the loanword friend is exactly the escape hatch Lesson 2 offered. Five lessons in, the pieces recombine into real description.

Note also that dāni for a female classmate is grammatically standard but informal — fine among peers, not something to use about a colleague. āme would be the neutral choice there.

Quick self-test

Cover the green line first. Several of these ask you to pick a register, not just a form.

  • Ask a stranger their name.mī pēru ēmiṭi?   (stranger → always mī, never nī)
  • Say that question the way it actually sounds at speed.mī pēreṇṭi?   (pēru + ēnti — the vowel gets deleted)
  • "His name is Sanjay" — neutral register.atani pēru Sanjay.   (atanu → atani)
  • "Her name is Divya" — neutral register.āme pēru Divya.   (āme doesn't change)
  • "His name" using the formal pronoun.āyana pēru.   (unchanged)
  • Which two possessives are completely new words?dāni (from adi) and vāṭi (from avi) — both neuter.
  • "Their names" for a group of people.vāḷḷa pērlu.   (vāḷḷu → vāḷḷa, and pēru → pērlu)
  • "Our names" — you're telling someone outside your group.mā pērlu.   (exclusive — they're not included)
  • Now say it including your listener.mana pērlu.   (inclusive)
  • Which possessives are identical to their pronouns?āyana, āviḍa, āme.

mī pēru ēmiṭi? — you can now ask anyone's name respectfully, and describe a whole room of people using the right possessive for each relationship and register.
