Past tense — and the stem you have to memorise

Structurally this is Lesson 8 with one piece swapped: -tunnā- becomes -ā- and the person markers are unchanged. The catch is the stem. The past is the one tense where a verb's stem is often unpredictable from both its root and its present tense — chaduvu gives chaduvutunnānu but chadivānu. This is the paradigm to over-learn.

How I processed this

Videos 13 and 14 merged, same as the last lesson: the second is the pronoun table, not new grammar.

Video 13's captions open with the word "Anarchy.", which is namaskāraṁ. It does not improve from there — āḍānu is "Adorno" and "Arduino", chēsānu is "chasanow", vachchānu is "Vatsyayana", paḍukunnānu is "Paducah Nano", and rātri match chūsānu is "Ratira Matsuzono". Full list at the bottom.

Every form here was generated from the Verb Lab stems and checked against tools/te2rom.py. Nineteen of them are directly attested in the videos and were used to audit the generator — see below.

The grammar engine

Structures & rules introduced

Colour code: past stem · tense marker · person marker. Most of this lesson is a substitution into machinery you already have. Rules 3 and 6 are the genuinely new work.

Swap the middle piece, keep the endings

The person markers are identical to the present continuous. Only the tense slot changes, and in the past it is barely there — just a long ā.

āḍ-past stem + -ā-past + -nuI → āḍānu

Compare: āḍu·tunnā·nu "I am playing" against āḍ·ā·nu "I played". Learning a new tense in Telugu costs one morpheme, not a new table. That is the payoff for the effort you put into Lesson 8.

The ā is shorter than it looks

The instructor stops to flag this and it is worth heeding: the long vowel in -ānu is often pronounced closer to the vowel in English ant than to a full long ā. Written long, said short.

This matters because it is the only thing separating some past forms from other tenses in fast speech. Do not lengthen it artificially to be clear — that marks you as reading rather than speaking. Let it be casual and let context carry it.

The past stem is not the present stem

The video says the exceptions "are the same as the ones we saw in the previous lesson". For two of the four classes that is true, and for a third of the verb list it is false. Getting this straight now is the difference between a rule and a memorisation load you were not expecting.

Nine of the 35 verbs have a past stem you cannot derive. They are listed and marked in the next section. Everything else follows one of four rules.

Why this is the good news, not the bad news

Nine irregular stems is tiny. English has around 180 irregular past tenses and you absorbed all of them. The reason Telugu feels harder is only that you are meeting them as a list instead of one at a time over six years.

The negative past is the root plus lēdu — and it ignores the stem entirely

After all that: the negative does not use the past stem. It attaches lēdu to the plain negative stem, which is close to the root.

chadava-+ lēdu→ chadavalēdu “didn’t read”

So chadivānu "I read" but chadavalēdu "I didn't read"; vachchānu "I came" but rālēdu "I didn't come". The irregular stems disappear in the negative. If you are unsure of a past form, the negative is the safe one.

lēdu is invariant across all eight pronouns, exactly as in Lesson 8.

The negative past and the negative present are one syllable apart

You met this warning last lesson; here is where it becomes live, because now you can build both.

TeluguEnglishBuilt from
pāḍalēdu పాడలేదుI didn't singnegative stem + lēdu
pāḍaḍaṁ lēdu పాడడం లేదుI'm not singingverbal noun + lēdu

Video 13 ends with exactly this four-way table for pāḍu, and it is the single most useful thing in the lesson:

PositiveNegative
Presentright nowpāḍutunnānu పాడుతున్నానుpāḍaḍaṁ lēdu పాడడం లేదు

Four cells. Drill them as a block for every verb you care about — this is now the whole of what you can say about an action, and it covers most of ordinary conversation.

Two verbs in one sentence: the second one becomes a noun

New syntax, and it reuses the -ḍaṁ you learned for the negative. When a sentence has two verbs, the main one goes last and the other becomes a verbal noun.

nēnu telugu māṭlāḍaḍaṁ nērchukunnānu నేను తెలుగు మాట్లాడడం నేర్చుకున్నాను
— I have learned to speak Telugu · literally "I learned Telugu speaking"

English says "learned to speak" with an infinitive. Telugu says "learned speaking" with a noun — the same -ḍaṁ form that pāḍaḍaṁ lēdu uses. One suffix, three jobs so far: negation, purpose (-ḍāniki), and now verb complements.

nēnu guitar vāyinchaḍaṁ nērchukunnānu — I have learned to play the guitar

This is the most productive single pattern in the lesson. Any "I want to ___", "I started to ___", "I forgot to ___" will be built this way.

Time words come before the object, and don't need "in"

Telugu word order inside the sentence is looser than the verb-final rule suggests, but adverbs of time have a normal home: right after the subject, before the object.

nēnu ninna yōgā chēsānu నేను నిన్న యోగా చేసాను — I did yoga yesterday

And there is no preposition. English needs "in the morning"; Telugu just uses the time word: podduna is both "morning" and "in the morning". Adding -lō here would be wrong.

yesterdayninnaనిన్న
morning / in the morningpoddunaపొద్దున
afternoonmadhyāhnaṁమధ్యాహ్నం
eveningsāyantraṁసాయంత్రం

"I came home" takes the -ki that English drops

English has an irregularity here that you have never noticed: you say "I went to the office" but "I came home", with no to. Telugu is regular — a destination always takes -ki.

nēnu iṇṭiki vachchānu నేను ఇంటికి వచ్చాను — I came home

Note the stem too: illu ఇల్లు is "house", but it becomes iṇṭi- before a case ending. Telugu nouns have oblique stems the way verbs do; iṇṭiki is the first one you meet, and it is high-frequency enough to learn whole.

Plurals in -lu

Slipped in without comment during the video-14 quiz: pāṭa పాట is "song", pāṭalu పాటలు is "songs". -lu is the general plural suffix.

Note what does not happen. vāḷḷu pāṭalu pāḍāru — the verb agrees with the subject (vāḷḷu, hence -ru), and the plural object changes nothing. Same as English, but worth confirming because Telugu marks so much else on the verb.

The class the video says carries over, and it does.

The video pauses on chūḍu → chūsānu — "why is the da sound replaced by a sa sound?" No reason you can use; it is just what this class does, and it does it in every tense.

Nasal · the stem doubles its n

Where the present went retroflex (tiṇṭunnānu), the past geminates instead. Different mechanism, same trigger.

eat · tinutinnānuతిన్నాను
hear · vinuvinnānuవిన్నాను
say · anuannānuఅన్నాను
be, stay · uṇḍuunnānuఉన్నాను
unnānu does double duty unnānu is the past of uṇḍu and the ordinary way to say "I am (here / located)". nēnu iṇṭlō unnānu is "I'm at home", present tense. Context separates them, and this is the one place in the tense system where that is genuinely ambiguous.

The she/it row eats the tense marker

Every other cell is stem + ā + marker. This one is stem + i + ndi — āḍindi, not *āḍādi. The same cell contracted in the present too (āḍutōndi, not *āḍutunnadi), for the same reason: it is the most-used cell in the language, because feminine and non-human share it.

Expect this cell to be irregular in every tense. That is now a prediction you can carry into the future tense rather than a surprise.

The part with no English equivalent

Telugu has no perfect tense — and doesn't need one

English distinguishes "I studied engineering" from "I have studied engineering". Telugu uses one form for both, and lets context do the work. This is the most useful thing in the lesson for a learner who is over-thinking.

TeluguBoth of these
nēnu injanīring chaduvukunnānu
నేను ఇంజనీరింగ్ చదువుకున్నాను		I studied engineering.
I have studied engineering.
nēnu telugu nērchukunnānu
నేను తెలుగు నేర్చుకున్నాను		I learned Telugu.
I have learned Telugu.
nēnu marketing lō pani chēsānu
నేను marketing లో పని చేసాను		I worked in marketing.
I have worked in marketing.

The instructor's reasoning is worth keeping: nobody learns a language or a degree yesterday, so the "have" reading falls out of what the sentence is about. Telugu leaves it to be inferred rather than marking it.

This confirms a decision made earlier in this project

The verb forms deep dive left the perfect out of the Verb Lab on the grounds that "Telugu's perfect is not where English's 'have done' goes — most of the time English 'I have eaten' is just the plain past, tinnānu. Generating a 'perfect' column would teach a false equivalence."

The course now says the same thing outright. Nothing to add to the Verb Lab. Stop looking for a perfect tense; you already have it.

The exercise the video sets you

Describe yesterday, broken down by time of day: podduna · madhyāhnaṁ · sāyantraṁ · rātri. Then go further back — where you have lived and worked, what you studied. Everything is the -ānu ending.

This is the best free-production prompt the course has given so far. Do it aloud in your daily block and write down what you could not say.

Quick self-test

Cover the green line and say each aloud.

  • What changes between āḍutunnānu and āḍānu?Only the tense slot: -tunnā- becomes -ā-. The person marker -nu is untouched.
  • Past of chaduvu — and why can't you guess it?chadivānu. The present is chaduvutunnānu; the past shifts the vowel to i. Unpredictable.
  • Now negate it.chadavalēdu. The irregular stem disappears — the negative uses the plain stem.
  • Say "I came home in the evening."nēnu sāyantraṁ iṇṭiki vachchānu.
  • Why iṇṭiki and not illu?A destination takes -ki, and illu becomes the oblique stem iṇṭi- before an ending. English is the odd one for saying "came home" with no "to".
  • Give all four cells for pāḍu: am singing, am not singing, sang, didn't sing.pāḍutunnānu · pāḍaḍaṁ lēdu · pāḍānu · pāḍalēdu
  • "She played." Why isn't it *āḍādi?āḍindi. The she/it cell contracts in every tense — it's the most-used cell in the language.
  • Say "I have learned to speak Telugu."nēnu telugu māṭlāḍaḍaṁ nērchukunnānu. — second verb becomes a -ḍaṁ noun, main verb last.
  • How do you say "I have studied engineering" as opposed to "I studied engineering"?Identically. nēnu injanīring chaduvukunnānu. Telugu has no perfect tense.
  • Say "I drank tea in the morning" — watch for a word you should NOT add.nēnu podduna ṭī tāgānu. No -lō. The time word carries "in" by itself.
  • Your mother-in-law went home. Say it.... gāru iṇṭiki veḷḷāru. — gāru forces the -ru ending.
  • Which is easier to get right, the past positive or the past negative — and why?Negative. One invariant form (verb + lēdu) for all eight pronouns, and it ignores the irregular past stems.
  • Now describe your actual yesterday, four sentences, one per time of day.podduna ... / madhyāhnaṁ ... / sāyantraṁ ... / rātri ... — all ending -ānu.
