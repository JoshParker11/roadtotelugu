Two suffixes that carry the whole sentence

English strings nouns together with little words in front of them. Telugu glues endings on behind. The video's demonstration is the clearest way to see it — Raj school bus Pooja veḷḷāḍu means almost nothing until the endings arrive.

This first pair is not the easy set. -ki and -ni do not map onto English words, and one of them is behind the biggest structural difference between the two languages you have met so far.

How I processed this

The accusative pronouns are where the captions collapse: nannu / ninnu / mimmalni arrive as "Nunu / ninu / Memani", and the third-person set as "Ionone Vardhini Akkineni". Those were rebuilt from the possessive stems already in the masters rather than from the audio, and every form on this page is checked against tools/te2rom.py.

Part 2 — -lō, -tō, nunchi and the rest — is a separate video and will be a separate page. The instructor separates them deliberately: those behave like their English equivalents, and these two do not.

The idea

Behind the noun, not in front of it

WithoutWith
EnglishRaj went school bus PoojaRaj went to school by bus with Pooja
TeluguRaj skūl bas Pūja veḷḷāḍuRaj skūlki baslō Pūjatō veḷḷāḍu

Same three relations, same information. English puts them before the noun as separate words; Telugu welds them onto the end. That is the whole reason for the name: postpositions.

You have been using them since Lesson 8 without a name — -lō, -tō, -ki. This lesson is where two of them get examined properly.

The dative

-ki / -ku — motion, recipients, and more

Two spellings of one suffix. Written Telugu picks between them by what precedes; spoken Telugu treats them as interchangeable, with -ki commoner.

Telugu
nēnu iṇḍiyāki veḷtunnānu
నేను ఇండియాకి వెళ్తున్నాను		I'm going to India.destination
Rāj Gītaki giphṭ ichchāḍu
రాజ్ గీతకి గిఫ్ట్ ఇచ్చాడు		Raj gave Gita a gift.recipient
ḍākṭar gāru iṇṭiki vachchāru
డాక్టర్ గారు ఇంటికి వచ్చారు		The doctor came home.illu → iṇṭi-, from Lesson 9

On a pronoun, use the possessive stem — never the subject form

This is the rule that makes the pronoun tables below look strange, and it applies to every postposition, not just this one.

nā+-ku→nāku to me

Built on nā "my", not on nēnu "I". *nēnuku does not exist.

And the three that take -ku rather than -ki are exactly the ones built on a long ā: nāku, māku, manaku — plus nīku and mīku. Everything third-person takes -ki.

The part that is not English

In Telugu you cannot want something

"I like cricket" and "I want water" do not use nēnu. You are not the subject of liking or wanting — those things happen to you, and the sentence says so.

English thinksTelugu saysLiterally
I like cricketnāku krikeṭ aṇṭē iṣṭaṁto-me cricket is liked
I want waternāku nīḷḷu kāvālito-me water is wanted
I don't know Telugunāku telugu rāduto-me Telugu does not come
You have already been doing this since Lesson 10

nāku telugu rādu was presented then as a fixed phrase. It is not — it is this construction, and so are liking, wanting, knowing, being hungry, having a headache, and being cold. Telugu treats all of them as things that arrive at you rather than acts you perform.

Once you see the shape, a large family of sentences opens at once, and they all start nāku.

aṇṭē is the hinge, and it means almost nothing

You do not say *nāku krikeṭ iṣṭaṁ. You need aṇṭē between them — roughly "as for", "the thing about". The instructor's gloss is "to me, cricket is what I like".

It is the quotative ani again, in another guise — the same particle from Lesson 11. Fourth sighting of that family. Still filed, not taught.

nāku krikeṭ aṇṭē chālā iṣṭaṁ నాకు క్రికెట్ అంటే చాలా ఇష్టం — I like cricket very much

chālā for "very much", koncheṁ for "a little", and iṣṭaṁ lēdu to negate: nāku krikeṭ aṇṭē iṣṭaṁ lēdu.

Liking a person, and liking to do something

Same frame. When the thing you like is a pronoun, its final vowel is eaten by aṇṭē:

SlowlyAt speed
nāku nuvvu aṇṭē iṣṭaṁnāku nuvvaṇṭē iṣṭaṁ నాకు నువ్వంటే ఇష్టంI like you
nāku āme aṇṭē iṣṭaṁnāku āmaṇṭē iṣṭaṁ నాకు ఆమంటే ఇష్టంI like her

And to like doing something, drop in the -ḍaṁ verbal noun from Lesson 8:

nāku krikeṭ āḍaḍaṁ aṇṭē iṣṭaṁ నాకు క్రికెట్ ఆడడం అంటే ఇష్టం — I like playing cricket

Fourth job for -ḍaṁ: negation, purpose, verb complements, and now this.

kāvāli and vaddu — want and don't want

nāku oka glās nīḷḷu kāvāli నాకు ఒక గ్లాస్ నీళ్ళు కావాలి — I want a glass of water nāku nīḷḷu vaddu నాకు నీళ్ళు వద్దు — I don't want water

vaddu is the same word you learned in Lesson 3 for refusing an offer, and in Lesson 7 as the prohibitive. One word, three uses, all of them "no thanks" in some form.

The accusative

-ni marks who it happens to — so word order stops mattering

There is no English equivalent, because English does this job with position. Put the noun before the verb and it acts; put it after and it receives. Telugu marks it instead, which frees the order entirely.

TeluguScriptMeans
Gīta bālni koṭṭindiగీత బాల్‌ని కొట్టిందిGita hit the ball
Gītani bāl koṭṭindiగీతని బాల్ కొట్టిందిThe ball hit Gita

Read those two again. Gita comes first in both. The ball comes second in both. The verb is identical. The only thing that moved is the suffix — and the meaning reversed.

TeluguMeans
Rāj kukkani karichāḍu
రాజ్ కుక్కని కరిచాడు		Raj bit the dog.
Rājni kukka karichindi
రాజ్‌ని కుక్క కరిచింది		The dog bit Raj. — the likelier of the two
Rāj Gītani prēmistāḍu
రాజ్ గీతని ప్రేమిస్తాడు		Raj loves Gita.
Rājni Gīta prēmistundi
రాజ్‌ని గీత ప్రేమిస్తుంది		Gita loves Raj.
Put it on the wrong noun and you get nonsense, not an accent nēnu abbāyini skūlki pampānu is "I sent the boy to school". Swap the suffixes — nēnu abbāyiki skūlni pampānu — and you have said "I sent the school to the boy." Grammatical, parseable, and absurd. There is no version of this where the listener works out what you meant from context.
Why this is worth the effort

English speakers spend years learning that Telugu word order is "free". It is not free — it is marked. The order can move because the endings already say who did what, and that is a better system than English's, not a looser one.

It also explains something you have already seen: why the verb sits at the end and everything before it can shuffle. The endings hold the sentence together, so the order is available for emphasis instead.

Where to spend your attention

Four accusatives are suppletive and have to be learned outright: nannu, ninnu, mimmalni, mammalni. They do not come from the possessive the way everything else does.

Two datives are irregular too: adi → dāniki and avi → vāṭiki. The stems dāni- and vāṭi- appear from nowhere. Everything else in the table is its possessive plus an ending.

Quick self-test

  • Why is it nāku and not *nēnuku?Postpositions attach to the possessive stem, nā, not the subject form nēnu. True of every postposition.
  • "I like cricket." Do not use nēnu.nāku krikeṭ aṇṭē iṣṭaṁ. — "to me cricket is liked".
  • Why can't you say *nēnu krikeṭ iṣṭaṁ?You're not the subject of liking — it happens to you. Same shape as nāku telugu rādu.
  • "I like playing cricket."nāku krikeṭ āḍaḍaṁ aṇṭē iṣṭaṁ. — the -ḍaṁ verbal noun again, fourth job for it.
  • Ask for a glass of water, then refuse one.nāku oka glās nīḷḷu kāvāli. / nāku nīḷḷu vaddu.
  • Translate both: Gīta bālni koṭṭindi and Gītani bāl koṭṭindi.Gita hit the ball / The ball hit Gita. Word order identical — only the -ni moved.
  • "Gita loves Raj." Which noun gets -ni?Rājni Gīta prēmistundi. Raj receives the loving, so Raj takes -ni.
  • What does nēnu abbāyiki skūlni pampānu mean?"I sent the school to the boy." Suffixes swapped — grammatical and absurd.
  • Object forms of "me", "you", "you formal".nannu, ninnu, mimmalni. Suppletive — they don't come from the possessive.
  • "to it" and "to those".dāniki and vāṭiki. Irregular stems dāni- and vāṭi-.
  • Say three things you like and one you don't, about your real life.nāku ___ aṇṭē iṣṭaṁ ×3, nāku ___ aṇṭē iṣṭaṁ lēdu.
