Family & kinship names

English collapses a dozen distinct relationships into "uncle," "aunt," and "cousin." Telugu does not. Kinship terms encode which side of the family and relative age, and the joint-family household is why that precision matters. The good news: most of these terms are transparent compounds you can build rather than memorise.

Two axes English throws away

Telugu kinship terms are specified along two dimensions that English ignores entirely:

Which side? Your mother's brother and your father's brother are different words. Relative age? Your mother's elder sister and younger sister are different words.

The lesson ties this to the joint family system — multiple generations under one roof means these distinctions carry real daily information about seniority, authority, and who you address how. A word like "uncle" would be uselessly vague in a household where the specific relationship determines the register you use.

Why this matters for you You do not need to produce all of these to benefit. Recognising peddamma versus pinni in a conversation tells you the family structure being described — that is comprehension value long before it is production value.

Siblings split by age — there is no generic "brother"

Four words where English has two. You cannot say "sister" without committing to elder or younger.

ElderYounger
sisterakka అక్కchelli చెల్లి
brotherannayya అన్నయ్యshortens to annatammuḍu తమ్ముడు

These are also used as address terms well beyond actual siblings — calling a slightly older man anna is common and warm. That's a Hyderabad street-level usage worth knowing, though this lesson only covers the literal family sense.

Grandmothers are transparent compounds

You already know both halves from Lesson 2. The grandmother terms are literally mother's mother and father's mother, formed by sticking the words together.

ammamother + ammamother → ammamma
nānafather + ammamother → nānamma
ammamma అమ్మమ్మ — maternal grandmother (mother's mother)
nānamma నానమ్మ — paternal grandmother (father's mother)

Two words learned for free. This compounding logic is the single most useful thing in the lesson, and rule 05 extends it further.

Grandfather doesn't split

tāta తాత covers both grandfathers. After the careful maternal/paternal distinction on the grandmother side, this asymmetry is worth noticing rather than assuming you missed something.

tāta తాత — grandfather, either side

pedda and chinna build "elder" and "younger" kin

Two ordinary adjectives — pedda పెద్ద ("big, elder") and chinna చిన్న ("small, young") — attach to parent words to generate uncle and aunt terms. The literal translations are startling and make them easy to remember.

peddaelder + ammamother → peddamma
Built formLiterallyActually means
peddamma పెద్దమ్మelder motherMother's elder sister
peddanāna పెద్దనాన్నelder fatherFather's elder brother
chinnāna చిన్నాన్నyounger fatherFather's younger brother

The logic is that your parent's same-sex sibling is a kind of secondary parent — which is exactly how a joint household functions. Note that the mother's younger sister breaks the pattern with its own word, pinni పిన్ని, rather than a chinna- compound.

pedda and chinna are general-purpose adjectives, not kinship-only. You'll meet them again for physical size.

The paternal side

Mirror image: here the sister has one word and the brothers split by age.

RelationTermNote
Father's sister paternalatta అత్తSame word whether elder or younger

The symmetry is neat: on each side, the opposite-sex sibling of your parent gets one age-neutral word (māmayya, atta), while the same-sex sibling splits by age into a parent-compound.

gāru — the honorific for people

A new politeness tool, and importantly not the same as Lesson 3's aṇḍi. gāru గారు attaches to a person's name or kinship term to mark respect.

tātagrandfather + gāruhonorific → tāta gāru

The lesson gives a clean heuristic: the older the family member, the more likely you use it. Grandparents almost always take gāru; parents sometimes do; siblings generally don't.

SuffixAttaches toExample
gāru గారుNames and person nounstāta gāru, amma gāru, atta gāru
aṇḍi అండిGreetings, answers, phrasesavunandi, raṇḍi, vaddandi

For your purposes this is high-value: gāru after a colleague's name is one of the simplest respect markers available to you, and it works in otherwise-English sentences.

atta and māma do double duty

Look carefully at the in-law terms and you'll notice the same words from rules 06–07 reappearing:

TermAs blood relativeAs in-law
atta అత్తFather's sisterMother-in-law (atta gāru)
māma మామMother's brother (māmayya)Father-in-law (māma gāru)

This overlap is not a coincidence or an ambiguity. In traditional Dravidian kinship, marriage between cross-cousins was common, which would make your father's sister literally also your mother-in-law. The vocabulary preserves that older structure even where the practice has changed.

Practically: in-law terms take gāru essentially by default, which also disambiguates them in conversation.

Spouse terms: formal vs. colloquial

Lesson 2 gave you the formal pair. This lesson adds the everyday pair — and confirms what moguḍu actually means.

FormalColloquial / everyday
wifebhārya భార్యpellām పెళ్ళాం
husbandbharta భర్తmoguḍu మొగుడు
Flag resolved Lesson 2 presented moguḍu as "man," and I flagged it as suspicious. Confirmed here: it means husband. The colloquial pair is noticeably informal — with your in-laws or senior colleagues, the traditional mā āyana / mā āviḍa forms from Lesson 2 remain the safer choice.

The -ḍu / -rālu pattern returns

Lesson 2's gender pattern is all over this vocabulary. Once you spot it, the grandchild and in-law terms come in pairs.

Male · -ḍuFemale · -rālu / -aluMeaning
manavaḍu మనవడుmanavarālu మనవరాలుgrandson / granddaughter
alluḍu అల్లుడుkōḍalu కోడలుson-in-law / daughter-in-law
tammuḍu తమ్ముడు—younger brother

Note kōḍalu doesn't take the full -rālu ending — the pattern is a strong tendency, not an absolute rule. And remember from Lesson 2 that these -ḍu words shift to -ḍi before the person marker: nēnu alluḍini, "I am the son-in-law."

How the lesson suggests learning these

Don't memorise the grid — assign the names to your actual relatives. Start with the people you'd genuinely mention: your wife, her parents, your own parents, siblings. The terms attach to real faces, which is far stickier than a table, and it front-loads exactly the vocabulary you'd use in a real conversation with her family.

Quick self-test

Most of these require you to pick a side and an age, not just a word. Cover the green line first.

  • Your mother's elder sister.peddamma.   (pedda + amma — "elder mother")
  • Your mother's younger sister.pinni.   (not a compound — its own word)
  • Your father's younger brother.chinnāna.   (chinna + nāna — "younger father")
  • Your father's sister — and does her age matter?atta.   No — age-neutral, like māmayya on the other side.
  • Your mother's mother, built from its parts.amma + amma → ammamma.   Usually ammamma gāru.
  • Elder sister and younger sister.akka / chelli.
  • Address your grandfather respectfully.tāta gāru.   (gāru attaches to people, aṇḍi to phrases)
  • Your mother-in-law and father-in-law.atta gāru / māma gāru.   Same roots as the paternal aunt and maternal uncle.
  • Son-in-law and daughter-in-law.alluḍu / kōḍalu.
  • "I am the son-in-law." — mind the Lesson 2 sound rule.nēnu alluḍini.   (-ḍu → -ḍi before the person marker)
