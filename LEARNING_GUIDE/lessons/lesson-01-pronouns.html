Pronouns & the missing "to be"

Everything Lesson 1 introduced, split into the two things you're collecting: the rules of how words fit together, and an Anki-ready word list in your exact five-field format. The lesson's big idea: Telugu has no "is/am/are" — you just place two words side by side and attach a tiny person marker to the predicate.

The grammar engine

Structures & rules introduced

Colour code throughout: subject pronoun · predicate word · person marker / suffix. The lesson notes that all future Telugu grammar is built by modifying pronoun sounds — so this is the foundation everything else stacks on.

There is no "to be"

Telugu has no copula (no is / am / are) and no definite article (the). To say "Raj is a student," you place the two words together and stop. The literal shape is just "Raj student."

Rajsubject vidyārthistudent
Raj vidyārthi.   — "Raj is a student."

oka — “one” with an article-like job

Telugu does not require an indefinite article. Oka is the before-a-noun form of “one,” and it can also introduce “a” particular person or thing. Use the bare predicate Rāj vidyārthi as the neutral “Raj is a student”; adding oka deliberately makes Raj one member/example of the category.

Raj oka vidyārthi.   — "Raj is a student."

Person markers on the predicate

Here's the twist. For 1st and 2nd person subjects, you attach a small person marker to the predicate word, reinforcing who the sentence is about. Only four pronouns take one; every third-person pronoun takes none.

nēnuI vidyārthistudent + -ni"I" marker → vidyārthini
nēnu vidyārthini.   — "I am a student."

Plurals: add -lu / -ulu

Make almost any noun plural by adding -lu or -ulu — the direct equivalent of English -s/-es. This includes English loanwords.

vidyārthi → vidyārthulu  ·  kāru → kārlu  ·  champion → championlu

Vowel elision when suffixes attach

Two vowels can't sit together. When a word ending in a vowel meets a suffix starting with a vowel, the first vowel is deleted. This is why "we are students" isn't vidyārthulu-amu but vidyārthulamu — the final u of the plural gets eaten by -amu.

vidyārthulustudents + amu"we" marker → vidyārthulamu
manam vidyārthulamu.   — "We are students." (colloquially often -am)

Two "we": inclusive vs exclusive

Telugu distinguishes whether the listener is included. manamu = inclusive ("you and I"); mēmu = exclusive ("us, but not you"). Saying "we're going" to your father-in-law with manamu invites him along; with mēmu you're just informing him.

Two "you": intimacy vs default politeness

nuvvu (informal) is for the closest friends and family only — anywhere else it can read as rude. mīru is both the plural and the respectful singular. When unsure, always default to mīru. This is exactly your in-law register.

Third person comes in formality tiers

"He" and "she" each have three forms by formality. Pick the neutral one when in doubt; it works almost everywhere. The informal forms (vāḍu, adi) carry the same caution as nuvvu — intimates only.

"They" splits by human vs non-human

For people, "they" is vāḷḷu (note the retroflex ḷḷ — tongue curled back). For objects and non-humans, "they" is avi. They are not interchangeable. Also note adi ("it") shares its form — and grammar — with the informal "she," but only in the singular.

Near vs distant: the a → i / e shift

Every third-person pronoun above is the distant form: the person or thing is farther from the speaker, like English that or those. Its near partner points to someone or something close to the speaker, like this or these.

The pattern is an opening a / ā sound → i / ī sound. Short stays short (a → i), and long stays long (ā → ī). The register and human/non-human meaning do not change.

Loanwords get a -u tail

English words are absorbed by adding a -u ending: car → kāru, and then pluralized normally: kārlu. The lesson uses "champion" and "teacher" directly this way — handy, since you can lean on English nouns you don't yet know in Telugu.
