Introducing yourself & the my / our split

Lesson 1 gave you the identity frame. Lesson 2 fills it with real content about you — name, age, profession, nationality, hometown, and family — and introduces the possessives nā (my) and mā (our). The cultural headline: close family always takes mā, "our," even if you are an only child.

How I processed this

Auto-generated captions again, and this transcript was rougher than Lesson 1's — it rendered abbāyi as "abi," āḍadi as "Ardhangini," pēru as "Paru," iravai as "Airway," and bhārya as "Bahia." I reconstructed each from context and normalized everything to the same ISO-style diacritics used across the site.

Two items are worth confirming with your wife before you drill them: moguḍu for "man" (see the flag in rule 04) and whether the course's mātṛbhāṣa line was "mother tongue" as I've read it. Everything else is standard and low-risk.

The grammar engine

Structures & rules introduced

Same colour code as Lesson 1: subject / possessor · predicate word · person marker / suffix. Lesson 2 mostly reuses the Lesson 1 machine — it adds possessives, a gender pattern, and one sound rule that explains a lot of what you'll hear.

nēnu has a long vowel

The lesson opens by correcting pronunciation before anything else. It is nēnu నేను with a long ē, not a short "nenu." Vowel length is contrastive in Telugu, so this is not a cosmetic detail — it is the difference between being understood immediately and making your listener work.

nēnu నేను   — hold the ē. Same for pēru, not "peru."

The identity frame now takes any category

Lesson 1's frame was nēnu + NOUN + -ni. Lesson 2 shows that this one pattern covers everything you'd say about yourself: gender, species, profession, nationality. Learn the frame once and you swap the middle word forever.

nēnuI manishiperson + -ni"I" marker → manishini
nēnu abbāyini. నేను అబ్బాయిని. — "I am a boy."
nēnu ammāyini. నేను అమ్మాయిని. — "I am a girl."
nēnu manishini. నేను మనిషిని. — "I am a person / a human being."

Fast speech deletes a vowel between words

This is the lesson's most useful listening rule. When a word ending in a vowel is followed by a word starting with a vowel, the first vowel gets swallowed at conversational speed. nēnu + abbāyini is not pronounced as two clean words — it collapses to nēnabbāyini.

You saw this inside a word in Lesson 1 (vowel elision when a suffix attaches). Here it happens across the word boundary. Expect it constantly: it is why native speech sounds faster than the words on the page.

nēnuI + abbāyiniboy + marker → nēnabbāyini
nēnu ammāyini → nēnammāyini నేనమ్మాయిని
nēnu āḍadini → nēnāḍadini నేనాడదిని

Stem-final -u becomes -i before the marker

Nouns ending in -ḍu or -lu don't take -ni directly. The final u shifts to i first, then the marker attaches. The lesson calls this a quirk of pronunciation; treat it as a reliable mechanical step.

bhāratīyuḍuIndian (m) → bhāratīyuḍistem shift + -ni"I" → bhāratīyuḍini
bhāratīyurālu → bhāratīyurālini — "I am an Indian woman."
moguḍu → moguḍini  ·  āḍadi → āḍadini — words already ending in -i just add -ni.
Resolved in Lesson 4 The course gives moguḍu మొగుడు as “man” here, which looked wrong — and Lesson 4 confirms it: moguḍu means husband, the colloquial counterpart to formal bharta. For “a man” in the general sense, use magavāḍu మగవాడు. Don't drill “nēnu moguḍini” as “I am a man.”

Male -ḍu / female -rālu

A pattern that runs through the whole language: many person-nouns come in a masculine form ending -ḍu and a feminine form ending -rālu. Once you notice it, you get the second half of every pair almost free.

MeaningMale · -ḍuFemale · -rālu
teacherupādhyāyuḍu ఉపాధ్యాయుడుupādhyāyurālu ఉపాధ్యాయురాలు
doctorvaidyuḍu వైద్యుడుvaidyurālu వైద్యురాలు
friendsnēhituḍu స్నేహితుడుsnēhiturālu స్నేహితురాలు
Indianbhāratīyuḍu భారతీయుడుbhāratīyurālu భారతీయురాలు

English profession words are normal, not lazy

The lesson makes a point of this: conversational Telugu freely uses English profession names, and for some jobs the Telugu word is effectively dead in speech. abhiyanta అభియంత ("engineer") is the example given — nobody says it; everyone says engineer.

The reasoning offered is historical: Telugu used to borrow technical vocabulary from Sanskrit the way English borrowed from Latin. English now occupies that slot. Indian professional settings are multilingual, so the loanword is often the more natural choice, not a fallback.

nēnu enjinīrni. నేను ఇంజినీర్‌ని. — "I am an engineer." (the normal way)
nēnu ḍākṭarni. నేను డాక్టర్‌ని.  ·  nēnu lāyarni. — equally fine.

This is good news for you: you can talk about your profession today without waiting for the vocabulary. The Telugu forms are still listed below so you recognise them when they appear in formal or written contexts.

Two possessives: nā (my) and mā (our)

They sit directly in front of the noun. No ending changes on either word, and nothing else is needed.

nāmy pēruname → nā pēru

Their standalone forms — mine and ours — add -di: nādi నాది and mādi మాది. You'll meet mādi again in rule 12, where it does the work of a whole sentence.

Lesson 5 sharpens this: mā is specifically the exclusive “our,” and mana మన is the inclusive one — the same split as mēmu / manamu from Lesson 1.

There is no apostrophe-s — just stack the words

Telugu has no equivalent of 's. "My doctor's name" is simply my + doctor + name, in that order, with nothing joining them. Possession is expressed by adjacency.

nāmy ḍākṭardoctor pēruname → nā ḍākṭar pēru
nā ḍākṭar pēru Sophia. నా డాక్టర్ పేరు సోఫియా. — "My doctor's name is Sophia."

Close family takes mā, not nā

This is the cultural core of the lesson. For immediate family — mother, father, siblings — Telugu speakers say our, not my. You do not say nā amma; you say mā amma, literally "our mother."

The reasoning given: the family, not the individual, is the central unit. So it is the family's mother and the family's father. This holds even if you are an only child — the "our" is not about siblings, it's about the family being the thing that owns the relationship.

mā amma మా అమ్మ — "my mother" (literally "our mother")
mā nāna మా నాన్న — "my father" (literally "our father")

People outside the family unit work the ordinary way, with nā: your friend, your doctor, your teacher.

nā snēhituḍu నా స్నేహితుడు — "my friend" (male)
nā ḍākṭar — "my doctor"
Why this matters for you Getting this wrong isn't a grammar error — it lands as a small cultural tell. Saying nā amma is intelligible but marks you as translating from English. mā amma is one of the cheapest ways to sound like you actually learned Telugu rather than decoded it.

Spouses take nā — with a traditional alternative

Husband and wife break the family pattern: they take nā. But the lesson offers a second, more traditional option that reuses the respectful pronouns from Lesson 1.

EverydayTraditionalLiterally
my wifenā bhārya నా భార్యmā āviḍa మా ఆవిడ"our respected she" — the matriarch of the family
my husbandnā bharta నా భర్తmā āyana మా ఆయన"our respected he" — the patriarch of the family

Note what just happened: āyana and āviḍa — the formal third-person pronouns you learned in Lesson 1 — have become nouns here. That kind of reuse is exactly why the pronoun set was worth learning properly.

Name and age use the same bare frame

No verb, no copula — Lesson 1's zero-copula rule doing the work again. Possessor, noun, value.

nā pēru Raj. నా పేరు రాజ్. — "My name is Raj."
nā vayasu iravai. నా వయసు ఇరవై. — "My age is 20." (iravai = 20, muppai = 30)

Numbers get their own lesson later; for now you only need the one that applies to you.

Origin: mā ūru → contracted to mādi

Hometown is family territory, so it takes mā: mā ūru Hyderabad, "our town is Hyderabad." In practice this contracts to mādi Hyderabad — literally "ours is Hyderabad," using the standalone possessive from rule 07.

mā ūru Hyderabad. మా ఊరు హైదరాబాద్. — "Our town is Hyderabad."
mādi Hyderabad. మాది హైదరాబాద్. — "Ours is Hyderabad." (the common form)

The same frame scales to any level of origin — city, region, state, country. mādi London, mādi Karnataka, mādi America.

Nationality: two routes to the same fact

You can state nationality with the identity frame or with the origin frame, and both are normal.

nēnu bhāratīyuḍini. నేను భారతీయుడిని. — "I am an Indian." (male)
mādi India. — "Ours is India." (same information, family framing)

Most country names are borrowed from English with minor pronunciation shifts, so nēnu Amerikanni / mādi America works immediately. India has a native name: Bhārata Dēśam భారత దేశం, where Bhārata is the ancient name and dēśam means "country." Both Bhārata Dēśam and India are used interchangeably.

The single most error-prone thing in this lesson. When in doubt, ask: is this person part of my family unit?

Loanword escape hatch

The lesson points out that nā friend is perfectly acceptable and sidesteps the male/female split entirely — snēhituḍu vs snēhiturālu becomes one word you can't get wrong. Worth knowing while the gender pairs are still settling.
