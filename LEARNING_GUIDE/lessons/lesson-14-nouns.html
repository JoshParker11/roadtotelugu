Nouns — and the two words that classify them

Ninety-three nouns across colours, fruit, vegetables, groceries, animals and flowers. The videos are word lists with no explanation, so everything on this page beyond the list is the pattern underneath it — and there is a good one.

Telugu does not split produce into fruit and vegetables. It splits it into kāya and paṇḍu, and the line falls somewhere English speakers do not expect.

How I processed this

No audio to reconstruct this time — the source is written, which brings a different problem. Three entries arrived with their first letter missing: garlic as ెల్లుల్లి (should be వెల్లుల్లి), bread as ్రెడ్ (బ్రెడ్), noodles as ూడిల్స్ (నూడిల్స్). A leading consonant had been dropped, leaving an orphan vowel sign. Repaired.

Every romanization here is re-derived from the script rather than taken from the source, which corrected sixteen spellings — paṇḍu not "panḍu", drākṣa not "Drāksha", chekka not "ceḵka". Water was written నీళ్లు; the standard spelling is నీళ్ళు.

One I am not sure of: papad as పాపడు. The usual Telugu is appaḍaṁ అప్పడం. Flagged rather than changed.

The pattern the videos don't mention

kāya and paṇḍu

Fifteen of these nouns are compounds ending in one of two words. Which one you get is not arbitrary.

paṇḍu పండు — the ripe thing

mangomāmiḍi paṇḍu
bananaaraṭipaṇḍu
guavajāmapaṇḍu
pomegranatedānimma paṇḍu

kāya కాయ — the unripe thing

brinjalvankāya
okrabeṇḍakāya
bitter gourdkākarakāya
watermelonpuchchakāya
The same plant gives you both

māmiḍi paṇḍu మామిడి పండు is a ripe mango, sweet, eaten as fruit. māmiḍikāya మామిడికాయ is a green mango — sour, and what goes into pickle. Same fruit, different word, because Telugu names the state rather than the species.

This is why watermelon is puchchakāya despite being sweet: it is a gourd, and gourds are named as kāya. English's fruit/vegetable line is culinary. Telugu's is about ripeness and plant type, and the two lines cross.

A third compound works the same way and needs no explanation: puvvu పువ్వు, "flower". Every flower on the list is X puvvu — gulābi puvvu, malle puvvu, tāmara puvvu. Learn puvvu and each flower costs you one word, not two. The same trick works for gōbhi puvvu, cauliflower — literally "cabbage flower".

Connecting to what you know

What these nouns need from the grammar

A vocabulary list is inert until it meets a sentence frame. You already have the frame — this is the lesson that makes Lesson 12 pay off.

Every noun here is neuter — so they all take undi / uṇṭundi

Objects, animals, food. That means the whole list plugs straight into the split you learned last lesson:

ṭamāṭa erupugā undi టమాట ఎరుపుగా ఉంది — this tomato is red (look at it) ṭamāṭa erupugā uṇṭundi టమాట ఎరుపుగా ఉంటుంది — tomatoes are red (as a rule)

That is the sentence frame this lesson unlocks, and it generates hundreds of true sentences: noun + colour·gā + uṇṭundi. Say ten of them about your kitchen tonight.

Colours are nouns, and -gā makes them describe

erupu is not "red", it is redness. telupu is whiteness, nalupu blackness. Adding -gā — the same suffix from Lesson 12 — turns each into the description.

erupuredness + -gā → erupugā undi

So the colour list is not eight new adjectives. It is eight nouns joining a machine you already own.

Nine of these are grammatically plural — and the verb has to agree

This is the one that will trip you. nīḷḷu ("water") is plural in Telugu. So is milk, and most of the things that come in grains or pods.

waternīḷḷuనీళ్ళు
milkpāluపాలు
eggsguḍluగుడ్లు
dalpappuluపప్పులు
peasbaṭānīluబటానీలు
peppercornsmiriyāluమిరియాలు
cardamomyālakuluయాలకులు
mustard seedāvāluఆవాలు
nīḷḷu challagā unnāyi నీళ్ళు చల్లగా ఉన్నాయి — the water is cold

Not *nīḷḷu challagā undi. The verb takes the avi ending -āyi — the neuter-plural row you met in Lesson 13 and have not needed until now. This is where that row starts earning its place.

Four words doing two jobs

Not coincidences — each pair is one word whose two senses are obviously related once you see them.

WordWhy
pasupu పసుపుyellowturmericTurmeric is the yellow thing. The spice named the colour.
gulābī గులాబీpinkroseFrom Persian gulāb. English did the same with "rose" → "rosy".

Which words stayed English tells you a small history

ṭamāṭa, kyāreṭ, kābēji, āpil, breḍ, chiken, maṭan, nūḍils — all loans. vankāya, beṇḍakāya, kākarakāya, gummaḍikāya — all native.

The split is not random: produce that arrived with colonial trade kept its English name, and what was already growing there did not. Useful as a guessing heuristic — if a vegetable feels European, try the English word with a Telugu ending.

Quick self-test

Mostly about the patterns, not the words — the words are a list, the patterns are the lesson.

  • What is the difference between māmiḍi paṇḍu and māmiḍikāya?Ripe mango vs green mango. paṇḍu = ripened, kāya = unripe. Same plant, different word.
  • Why is watermelon puchchakāya when it's sweet?It's a gourd, and gourds take kāya. Telugu's line is ripeness and plant type; English's is culinary. They cross.
  • Say "this tomato is red" and "tomatoes are red".ṭamāṭa erupugā undi. / ṭamāṭa erupugā uṇṭundi. — observation vs standing fact.
  • What does erupu literally mean, and what turns it into "red"?Redness. -gā makes it a description — same suffix as Lesson 12.
  • "The water is cold." Careful with the verb.nīḷḷu challagā unnāyi. — nīḷḷu is plural, so -āyi, not undi.
  • Name three more nouns that take the plural verb.pālu, guḍlu, pappulu, baṭānīlu, miriyālu, yālakulu, āvālu.
  • You know puvvu. How many flower names does that give you?All of them — each is X puvvu. gulābi, malle, kaluva, sūryakānti, tāmara, banti, mandāra.
  • Two meanings of pasupu, and why they're the same word.Yellow and turmeric. The spice named the colour.
  • You've forgotten the Telugu for "beetroot". What's your best guess and why?Try the English word. Produce introduced by colonial trade kept English names — ṭamāṭa, kyāreṭ, kābēji, āpil.
  • Describe five things in your kitchen right now, aloud.noun + colour·gā + undi (looking at it) or uṇṭundi (in general). Watch the plurals.
