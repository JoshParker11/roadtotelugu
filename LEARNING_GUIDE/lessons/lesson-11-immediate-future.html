“About to” — and the one place Telugu copies English

The instructor opens by saying this is "not exactly a different tense", and he is right. -bōtunnānu is the verb pō ("go") fused onto the end of another verb, with the p softening to b. It is literally "I am going to ___".

English built its future the same way, out of the same verb, for the same reason. This is the only structure in the course so far where the two languages independently arrived at the identical metaphor — so it is also the least effort per unit of new expressive power you will get all week.

How I processed this

Short video, and the captions held up better than videos 16 and 19 — but -bōtunnānu still came through as "both nanu" throughout, which reads as English and is easy to skim past. chūḍabōtunnānu became "Kubota Nano" and "turabo nano", nērchukōbōtunnānu became "Near Kabutar Nanu", and dhanyavādālu signs off as "The Nevada blue".

One stretch collapses entirely — "The beauty to do yoga usefully. Verb. Other words." — and I reconstructed it from the surrounding examples rather than the audio.

A naming note. The course titles this "future continuous". That is not what it is: a future continuous would be "I will be playing". This is the immediate future (or prospective) — "about to", "going to". The instructor's own explanation says so; only the title disagrees. Named accurately here and in the Verb Lab.

The grammar engine

Structures & rules introduced

Colour code: plain stem · the fused verb · person marker. Three rules, and the first one explains the other two.

The suffix is a whole verb that got glued on

You already know pōtunnānu — "I am going", the present continuous of pō from Lesson 8. Attach it to another verb and the p softens to b, which is what Telugu does routinely when a word becomes a suffix.

āḍa-plain stem + bōtunnānupōtunnānu, softened → āḍabōtunnānu

Nothing here is new. You are gluing a form you already know onto a stem you already know. That is why this lesson is eleven minutes and the last one was twenty-one.

English did exactly this

"I am going to eat" is not about going anywhere either — it is the verb go bleached into a future marker, which is why it contracts to "gonna". Telugu bleached the same verb into the same suffix and softened it to -bō- instead of contracting it.

So the mental model is free: whenever you would say "I'm going to ___" in English, Telugu uses this form. Very little else in the course transfers this cleanly.

The person endings are Lesson 8's, untouched

Because the suffix is a present continuous, the endings are the present continuous endings. You are not learning a fourth set.

The she/it cell contracts to -tōndi, exactly as it did in Lesson 8 and for the same reason. Third tense in a row where that cell behaves the same way — by now it should be a reflex rather than an exception.

It uses the plain stem — the one every negative uses

This is the rule that actually needs learning, and the video states it twice.

Every irregular stem you have fought with disappears. chēyi stays chēya-; rā stays rā-; the -ō verbs keep their ō instead of turning into -uṇ-.

This is the same stem as all three negatives āḍanu · āḍalēdu · āḍaḍaṁ lēdu · āḍabōtunnānu. Four forms, one stem. Telugu keeps its irregularity in the positive non-past and the past, and everything else builds on something close to the root. Learn the plain stem for a verb and you get most of its paradigm for nothing.

Where the confusion actually is

Not with the immediate future — -bōtunnā- is four syllables long and unmistakable. The dangerous pair is still āḍutunnānu and āḍutānu, from Lessons 8 and 10: one syllable apart, both extremely common, and both about the present.

If anything, this lesson helps — -bōtunnā- contains -tunnā- visibly, which is a reminder that the continuous marker is the same piece everywhere it appears.

There is no negative for this one

The video does not teach one, and it is genuinely uncommon — to say you are not about to do something, Telugu reaches for the ordinary negative future āḍanu ("I won't play"). The Verb Lab therefore has no negative cell here either.

Worth one question to a native speaker: is there a natural way to say "I'm not about to ___", or does the language simply not make that distinction? Added to review/.

Slipped in without warning

The quotative ani — your first sighting

The last example in the video contains a word the course does not explain and will not return to for a long time. It is worth stopping on, because it is one of the genuinely major structures of the language.

nēnu ī rōju RRR ani sinimā chūḍabōtunnānu
నేను ఈ రోజు RRR అని సినిమా చూడబోతున్నాను
— I'm about to watch a movie called RRR today

ani is glossed here as "called / named", which is true but undersells it. ani is how Telugu quotes. It marks the material before it as something said, named, or thought, and it comes after that material rather than before.

StructureEnglishTelugu
Naminga movie called RRRRRR ani sinimā
Reported speechhe said that he's comingvastānu ani chepp​āḍu literally "I'll come" + ani + he said

Look at the second row carefully. Telugu has no word for "that" in the reported-speech sense. It quotes the original sentence verbatim, in the first person, and tags it with ani. "He said that he's coming" is built as "'I'll come,' he said".

Filed, not learned

The verb forms deep dive lists ani as one of the four big things waiting past the Verb Lab: "the quotative ani — how reported speech works. Common, very unlike English."

You now have one concrete example. Do not try to learn the system yet — memorise ___ ani as "called ___" and let the rest arrive when the course gets there. This is the second structure this week to show up early; vachchē in Lesson 10 was the first.

Quick self-test

Cover the green line and say each aloud.

  • Where does -bōtunnānu come from?pōtunnānu, "I am going" — the p softens to b when it becomes a suffix. Identical to English "going to".
  • "I'm about to do yoga." Watch the stem.nēnu yōgā chēyabōtunnānu. — chēya-, not chēsa-. This form never uses the s-stem.
  • Which other forms use that same stem?All three negatives: āḍanu, āḍalēdu, āḍaḍaṁ lēdu. Four forms, one stem.
  • Give all four: about to play, am playing, play/will play, played.āḍabōtunnānu · āḍutunnānu · āḍutānu · āḍānu
  • Which two of those four are actually easy to confuse — and why isn't it the new one?āḍutunnānu and āḍutānu. -bōtunnā- is four syllables and unmistakable.
  • "She's about to sing."āme pāḍabōtōndi. — the she/it cell contracts to -tōndi, third tense running.
  • "I'm about to sit down." Careful with the -ō.nēnu kūrchōbōtunnānu. — the ō survives here, unlike kūrchuṇṭunnānu.
  • Say "a movie called RRR" and explain the word order.RRR ani sinimā. — ani comes after the name, not before. It's the quotative.
  • How would Telugu build "he said that he's coming"?vastānu ani cheppāḍu — literally "'I'll come,' he said." No word for "that"; the original sentence is quoted verbatim in the first person.
  • "I'm about to write a letter" — and what else does that noun mean?nēnu uttaraṁ rāyabōtunnānu. uttaraṁ is also "north".
  • How do you say "I'm not about to play"?Trick question — the course teaches no negative here. Use āḍanu, "I won't play". Worth asking a native speaker whether the distinction exists.
  • Say three true things you are about to do in the next hour.nēnu ___ bōtunnānu. ×3 — use the plain stem every time.
