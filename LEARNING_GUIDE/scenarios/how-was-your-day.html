← Course home Scenario 01 · arriving at your in-laws'

“How was your day?”

Your mother-in-law will ask. This is a draft to react to, not a finished list — the point is to get something concrete in front of you so you can tell me what's wrong with it.

The good news first

You can already say most of this. bāgā and lēdu from Lesson 3, undi from Lesson 1, chālā, mīru, and attayya from Lesson 4. The handful of past-tense verbs below are memorised blocks — you're not expected to build them until Lesson 9, and I've flagged which ones those are.

Everything here is Telangana / Hyderabad form

Which mostly shows up as eṭla rather than elā. If your wife says her mother uses the other one, tell me and I'll flip the whole page — it's a mechanical change.

Step one

Recognise the question first

More important than the answer. If you don't catch what she asked, a perfect reply is useless — and she may not use the version you rehearsed. These are the realistic variants, roughly in order of likelihood.

most likely ఈ రోజు ఎట్ల గడిచింది? ī rōju eṭla gaḍichindi? How did today go? Listen for: rōju (day) and eṭla (how). Those two words are enough to identify it.
very likely ఏం చేసారు? ēṁ chēsāru? What did you do? Listen for: ēṁ (what). The -āru ending is the respectful "you" form — she's being polite to you.
likely బాగున్నారా? bāgunnārā? Are you well? Listen for: bāgu- plus a rising -ā at the end. That final -ā is the yes/no question marker — the same one you met on the song page.
near-certain తిన్నారా? tinnārā? Have you eaten? This one is coming whether you like it or not. See the follow-ups section — it matters more than the day question does.
She may use the informal “you” instead

Telugu families vary on this. Traditionally a son-in-law is given high respect and gets mīru with -āru endings. In a warm modern family she may well use nuvvu and say chēsāvu instead of chēsāru. Both mean the same thing — don't let the different ending throw you. Worth asking your wife which her mother will use, since it tells you how you're being positioned.

Step two

Four answers, increasing in ambition

Pick one and get it fluent before adding the next. A short answer delivered confidently beats a long one delivered haltingly — and in this particular exchange, short is also more natural. Nobody wants a report.

The one-word answer

If you learn nothing else on this page, learn this word. It answers almost any "how was X" question in Telugu.

use now బాగుంది bāgundi It was good. / It's good. Why you can already say this: it's bāgā ("well," Lesson 3) fused with undi ("it is," Lesson 1). Telugu doesn't need a separate past tense here — context carries it.
Careful: bāgundi vs bāgunnānu

If she asks about the day, answer bāgundi — the day is a thing, so it takes the neuter undi. If she asks about you (bāgunnārā?), answer bāgunnānu — "I am well," with the first-person ending from Lesson 1. Same root, different person marker. Getting these backwards is the most likely small error here.

Add warmth

Naming her is worth more than any extra vocabulary. It's the difference between answering a question and greeting a person.

use now చాలా బాగుంది అత్తయ్య chālā bāgundi attayya Very good, aunty. Why you can already say this: chālā ("very") plus attayya — the address form of atta from Lesson 4, the word that does double duty for aunt and mother-in-law.

Give one piece of content

One fact is plenty. This pattern is fully buildable from grammar you have — no past tense needed, because you're describing a state rather than an event.

use now ఈ రోజు ఆఫీసులో చాలా పని ఉంది ī rōju āphīsulō chālā pani undi There was a lot of work at the office today. Why you can already say this: -lō is the "in" ending, and undi is Lesson 1's "there is." Literally: today office-in much work is. No verb conjugation anywhere.
memorise whole కొంచెం అలిసిపోయాను konchaṁ alisipōyānu I'm a bit tired. Learn this as a block. alisipōyānu is past tense with a first-person ending — Lesson 9 territory. You'll take it apart later; for now it's one word. konchaṁ ("a little") is independently useful and softens almost anything.

Honest answers, for when it wasn't good

Worth having, because always saying "good" gets noticed. And parvālēdu may be the most useful single word on this page — it's the Telugu shrug.

use now పర్వాలేదు parvālēdu It was okay. / Not bad. / It's fine, don't worry about it. Enormously versatile. It also means "no problem" when someone apologises, and "it doesn't matter" when someone offers something you're declining. Card this one today.
use now ఈ రోజు కొంచెం కష్టం ī rōju konchaṁ kaṣṭaṁ Today was a bit hard. Why you can already say this: zero copula from Lesson 1 — no verb at all. Today a-little difficulty. kaṣṭaṁ is a high-frequency word worth knowing.
Make it generative, not memorised

Five fixed sentences will run out fast. This frame won't:

ī rōju PLACE-lō chālā THING undi
āphīsulō at the office intlō at home pani work meetings meetings samayaṁ time chālā a lot konchaṁ a little

And yes — say "meetings" in English. That's not a shortcut, it's what Hyderabad IT people actually do. Code-mixing is native here, and forcing a Telugu technical word would sound stranger than the English one.

Workshopped · two-clause answers

“It was good, and here's why”

The natural next step: a positive verdict plus one reason. Telugu handles this by simply putting the two clauses next to each other — no word for “and” is required, and inserting one would sound stilted.

chēsānu is a block బాగుంది, చాలా పని చేసాను bāgundi, chālā pani chēsānu It was good — I did a lot of work. The direct version. chēsānu is "I did" — past tense with the first-person -nu ending you know from unnānu. Memorise it whole for now; Lesson 9 explains the shape.
more natural బాగుంది, చాలా పని అయ్యింది bāgundi, chālā pani ayyindi It was good — a lot of work got done. This is the one I'd actually use. See the note below: Telugu reaches for "it got done" far more readily than "I did it," and in a family setting that framing lands better.
The impersonal preference — worth internalising early

“I got a lot done” is a very American sentence. It centres you as the agent, and English small talk rewards that. Telugu much more often puts the work in the subject position: pani ayyindi, literally “the work became.” The achievement still comes across; you just aren't standing in front of it.

Both versions are correct and neither will cause confusion. But ayyindi reads as more modest, and modesty about your own accomplishments is weighted more heavily in Telugu family conversation than in American. With a mother-in-law you're trying to build warmth with, that's the safer instinct.

pūrti ayyindi is a block బాగుంది, ఒక మంచి పని పూర్తి అయ్యింది bāgundi, oka manchi pani pūrti ayyindi It was good — a good piece of work got finished. Look at oka doing exactly its job. This is the deep-dive use: one newly introduced thing the listener doesn't know about yet. Drop it and the sentence goes vague. And manchi is the Lesson 3 “good” — the adjective form of manchidi.
“Cool” doesn't survive translation

There's no clean Telugu equivalent for “something cool” in the sense you mean — pleasing, clever, satisfying to have built. manchi (“good”) is the safe substitute and is what a Telugu speaker would use.

If you want the enthusiasm, the authentic Hyderabad move is to just say it in English: oka manchi project pūrti ayyindi, or even “it was really interesting” mid-sentence. Your in-laws will have heard far more English code-mixing than you'll produce. Reaching for an obscure Telugu word to avoid an English one is the mistake, not the other way round.

The traffic follow-up

This one's a gift — it's almost entirely buildable from grammar you already have, and it contains a genuine idiom correction.

use now కానీ దారిలో ట్రాఫిక్ చాలా ఎక్కువ ఉంది kāni dārilō ṭrāphik chālā ekkuva undi But there was a lot of traffic on the way. Only three new words. kāni (but), dāri (way, road), ekkuva (a lot, excessive). Everything else you have: -lō from āphīsulō, plus chālā and undi.
Don't say traffic was “bad”

This is the kind of thing that marks someone as translating rather than speaking. English rates traffic on a good/bad axis; Telugu rates it on quantity. You want ekkuva — “a lot,” “excessive.” Building chedda ṭrāphik (“bad traffic”) from a dictionary would be understood, but it isn't what anyone says.

The same instinct applies broadly: crowds, rain, work, and heat are all usually described by amount in Telugu rather than by quality.

Putting the whole thing together

Strung into one turn, which is a completely realistic thing to say at the door:

bāgundi attayya. chālā pani ayyindi. kāni dārilō ṭrāphik chālā ekkuva undi.

Three short sentences rather than one long one — which is both easier for you and more natural for her. Telugu conversation runs on short clauses far more than written English does, so don't feel you need to join them up.

Then hand it back: mīru eṭla unnāru?

Step three

What comes immediately after

The day question is small talk. It's the doorway to the conversation that actually matters, and you should be ready for where it goes.

The food question is not optional

In a Telugu household, tinnārā? — "have you eaten?" — functions as an expression of care, close to a greeting. It will be asked, probably within a minute of your arrival, possibly before the day question. Answering it well does more relationship work than anything else on this page.

Be aware that saying no reliably produces food, and that accepting food is itself the relationship move. Declining repeatedly reads as distance, not politeness.

chālu is the word to have ready

chālu means "enough / that's sufficient," and paired with a compliment it's how you stop a third helping without seeming to reject the hospitality. chālu, chālā bāgundi — "enough, it was very good" — does both jobs in three words.

Step four · the one that matters most

Ask her back

This is the highest-value thing on the page and it costs you one sentence. A learner who answers and stops has completed a transaction. A learner who answers and returns the question has started a conversation — and signalled that the language is for her, not homework.

use now మీరు ఎట్ల ఉన్నారు? mīru eṭla unnāru? How are you? Why you can already say this: mīru from Lesson 1, unnāru is the same existence verb with the respectful ending. The only new piece is eṭla.
use now ఇంట్లో అంతా బాగున్నారా? intlō antā bāgunnārā? Is everyone at home well? A very Telugu thing to ask. Enquiring after the whole household rather than the individual is standard and lands as warm. intlō is illu ("house") plus the -lō you used above.
memorise whole మీ రోజు ఎట్ల గడిచింది? mī rōju eṭla gaḍichindi? How did your day go? Straight mirroring. Take her question and swap in mī ("your," Lesson 5). Reusing someone's own construction back at them is the cheapest fluency trick there is, and it always fits.
The mirroring principle

Whatever she asks, you can nearly always hand it back by swapping the pronoun. You don't need to generate anything new — you just need to catch the question and change one word. Worth practising deliberately, because it makes a small vocabulary go a very long way.

Over to you

What I need from you to sharpen this

This is a first draft built on general Hyderabad usage. Your family is the authority, not me — and a few answers would let me tighten it considerably.

  • Does she use mīru or nuvvu with you? This changes every ending on the page. It also tells you something about how you're being positioned in the family, which is worth knowing independently.
  • What does she actually call you, and what do you call her? I've assumed attayya. Some families use atta, some add gāru, some use a name. Getting this right matters more than any sentence here.
  • Does she say eṭla or elā? You said Hyderabad style, so I've gone with eṭla throughout — but confirm, because it's on nearly every line.
  • Is the day question even the real one? In many households the actual opener is about food, traffic, or whether you're tired. If she never asks about your day, tell me what she does ask and I'll rebuild around that instead.
  • How much English does she use? Determines whether "meetings" and "office" stay in English or want Telugu equivalents.
One thing worth trying before you refine anything

Use bāgundi attayya once, exactly as it is. The reaction will teach you more than another round of drafting will — including whether the address form is right, which is the part I'm least sure of.
