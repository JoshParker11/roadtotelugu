Why it works this way

Not a table you fill in

You asked what the research says, so here it is, and here is what this site does with it.

01 / The finding

Retrieval beats completion

Copying a conjugation table while the answer is visible produces almost no durable learning. Pulling the form out of memory does. This is the single most replicated result in the learning literature, and it is why there is no fill-in-the-grid exercise anywhere on this site.

So: every drill item hides the answer until you have committed to one.

02 / The finding

Production, not recognition

Practice that makes you produce a form improves your ability to produce it later; practice that only makes you recognise one mostly improves recognition. Since the goal is speaking, multiple choice is the wrong instrument.

So: you say the whole form aloud, then grade yourself. Typing is optional and off by default.

03 / The finding

Interleaving beats blocking

Drilling one verb's whole paradigm in order feels productive and tests poorly a week later. Mixing verbs and forms feels worse in the moment and holds up far better, because it forces you to choose the pattern rather than repeat it.

So: the session avoids putting two items from the same verb in a row, including when it re-queues something you missed. Right at the end of a queue there is sometimes nothing else left to serve.

04 / The finding

Spacing, and errors with immediate feedback

Getting it wrong and being corrected at once beats being shown the right answer first. Items you miss come back inside the same session and again sooner on later days.

So: three grades — got it, shaky, missed — feed a spacing schedule kept in this browser.

The one thing rote tables are good for

You cannot retrieve a pattern you have never seen. The optional notice step shows each paradigm once at the start of a session with the stem in amber and the ending in orange, so the morphology is visible before you are asked to produce it. Look at it for twenty seconds, not five minutes — then drill. Turn it off once a verb is familiar.

An honest warning about the Telugu on this site

These paradigms are generated from a stem inventory I wrote. They are internally consistent, and a good number of individual forms cross-check against Telugu that already appears in your course — undi, unnānu, raṇḍi, tinalēdu, ivvaṇḍi, āgaṇḍi. None of it has been checked by a native speaker.

Four verbs are flagged check where I am not confident — run, throw, drive and fly — and the conditional is the least settled form throughout. Because the site stores stems rather than finished forms, getting it verified is a short conversation, not a proofreading job: the review page lays out roughly ten stems per verb, and one correction fixes that verb's entire paradigm at once.
