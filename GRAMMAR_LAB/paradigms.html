Every form, with the seams showing

Reference, not practice. Reading these tables will not teach you to produce the forms — that is what the drill is for. Come here to notice a pattern, settle an argument with yourself, or check what the drill just told you.

How to read them

Stem in amber, ending in orange

Telugu builds a verb by choosing a stem and attaching an ending. The endings never change from verb to verb; the stems do. Splitting them apart makes the whole system about a dozen facts plus a stem list.

The stems

Five per verb, at most

Non-past, past, negative, short-a and infinitive. Every form on this page is one of those five plus a fixed ending. Where a verb is irregular, it is irregular in its stems, not in its endings.

The endings

The same six, everywhere

-nu -vu -ḍu -di -ṁ -ru for the negative; -ānu -āvu -āḍu -undi -āṁ -āru after the tense marker. Learn them once and they apply to all 35 verbs.

The marker

t or ṭ

Non-past forms insert -t-, or -ṭ- after a nasal stem: chēs·t·ānu but tin·ṭ·ānu. That single choice accounts for most of what looks like irregularity at first.

The gaps

Some cells are empty on purpose

A dash means the form is not natural for that verb rather than missing — “let’s come” and “let’s fly” are not things people say. Better an honest gap than an invented form.
