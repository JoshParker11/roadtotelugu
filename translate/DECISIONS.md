# Decisions log

Append-only. One entry per call that was not obvious, with the reasoning. Standing rules that
apply everywhere belong in [STYLE.md](STYLE.md) instead; this is for the ones that came up while
translating a particular passage and will come up again.

Why keep it: the second time a construction appears, six weeks later, you will not remember what
you did the first time — and inconsistency is the failure mode a learner cannot self-detect.

Format:

```
## <date> — <short title>
**Context:** where it came up
**Decision:** what we did
**Why:** the reasoning
**Applies to:** how widely
```

---

## 2026-08-16 — Anchor terms stay in Latin script

**Context:** Setting the project up.
**Decision:** Names, spells, institutions, coined nouns, Quidditch roles, meaningful names and
genuinely culture-bound objects stay in English, in Latin script. Telugu morphology attaches to
them per STYLE.md §4.
**Why:** Anchor points. A published translation would transliterate them, and this deliberately
does not, because the reader is a beginner who needs to know where they are in a text they
already know. Naturalness is traded for navigability on purpose.
**Applies to:** Everything. This is the defining constraint of the project.

## 2026-08-16 — Three buckets, not two

**Context:** "Culture-bound objects" read broadly would swallow every noun in the book.
**Decision:** Anchor (Latin script) · transliterate (Telugu script) · translate (Telugu word).
Common nouns with no Telugu equivalent get *transliterated into Telugu script* — they do not
become anchors.
**Why:** A dragon is not a proper noun. If wand, cauldron, broomstick and cloak all stayed in
English there would be no Telugu left to learn, and generating that vocabulary is the point.
**Applies to:** Every common noun. See STYLE.md §3.

## 2026-08-16 — The official Telugu edition renders the Stone as పరుసవేది

**Context:** Sourcing a copy to check against.
**Decision:** Noted, not followed. `Sorcerer's Stone` stays an anchor.
**Why:** The published translator domesticated it — పరుసవేది is the real Telugu word for the
philosopher's stone. That tells us their policy sits much further toward translation than ours,
which is worth knowing when comparing. Our anchor policy is a study aid, not a competing
translation, so the divergence is expected rather than a mistake.
**Applies to:** All comparisons against the Manjul edition. Expect systematic divergence on
every proper noun; only compare the *Telugu around* the names.

- **`Vernon మామయ్య` / `Petunia అత్త` for Uncle Vernon and Aunt Petunia?** Recommended in
  STYLE.md §4 — keeps the anchor, teaches two very high-frequency kinship words. Confirm before
  it propagates through seventeen chapters.
- **The Vernon/Petunia address asymmetry.** ADDRESS.md call #1. Characterising, or dated?
- **`You-Know-Who` as an anchor.** It is a euphemism rather than a name, and anchoring keeps the
  taboo visible while losing the meaning entirely. A Telugu rendering would read better and
  break the anchor rule. Chapter 1 is where it appears most, so decide there.
- **Creature common nouns.** dragon, goblin, troll, centaur, unicorn — which get a Telugu word
  (ఏకశృంగి for unicorn is available) and which get transliterated? Chapter 1 needs none of them,
  but chapter 5 needs all of them.
- **Hagrid's dialect register.** Agreed in principle (STYLE.md §2), but how broad? Sample it in
  chapter 1 where he has few lines, before chapter 4 where he has many.
